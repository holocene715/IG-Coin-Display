This silver hemidrachm was struck in **Tabaristan**, a mountainous region along the southern coast of the Caspian Sea, during the reign of the Abbasid caliph **al-Mahdi (775–785 CE)**. Although issued under Islamic rule, the coin closely follows a monetary tradition inherited from the Sasanian Empire (see C010 for comparison). The obverse depicts a crowned royal bust facing right, surrounded by star-and-crescent motifs and inscriptions written in the Middle Persian **Pahlavi script**. The words *GDH AFZWT* form a traditional expression invoking the increase of royal glory, while additional words in the margins convey ideas of goodness or excellence.

The reverse preserves another distinctly Sasanian image: a **Zoroastrian fire altar flanked by two attendants**. Beside the altar are the mint and date, also written in Pahlavi. The mint inscription identifies **Tabaristan**, while the date records year **133 of the Post-Yazdigerd Era (PYE)**, corresponding to **784–785 CE**. This dating system counted from the death of the last Sasanian emperor, Yazdigerd III, providing another striking connection between the coinage of Islamic-period Tabaristan and the Sasanian past.

## Historical Significance

The Arab conquest brought most of the Sasanian Empire under Muslim rule during the seventh century, but **Tabaristan remained outside direct caliphal control for considerably longer**. Protected by the Alborz Mountains and the difficult terrain south of the Caspian Sea, the region was ruled by local Iranian dynasties that maintained traditions inherited from the Sasanians. In the early eighth century, the local Dabuyid rulers began striking silver coins based on Sasanian drachms, but at roughly half the traditional Sasanian weight. When Tabaristan was finally incorporated into the Abbasid state in the mid-eighth century, its new Muslim governors continued producing essentially the same local coinage.

C012 was struck during this Abbasid phase, more than **130 years after the death of Yazdigerd III and the collapse of the Sasanian Empire**. Yet almost every conspicuous feature of the coin remains rooted in pre-Islamic Iranian tradition: the crowned royal portrait, Zoroastrian fire altar, attendants, star-and-crescent imagery, Pahlavi script, and even a calendar whose starting point commemorated the end of Sasanian rule. The Abbasid governors did not simply copy old coins; they continued an established regional monetary system that remained useful and recognizable to the population of Tabaristan.

Tabaristan's silver coinage also participated in economic networks extending far beyond Iran. Sasanian, Arab-Sasanian, and Tabaristani silver coins have been recovered from **Viking Age hoards in Scandinavia and around the Baltic**, alongside the much larger quantities of Islamic dirhams that began flowing northward during the ninth and tenth centuries. These coins traveled through long-distance exchange networks connecting the Abbasid world with eastern Europe and the Baltic, particularly along the great river routes through what are now Russia and Ukraine. Silver moved north in exchange for commodities including **furs and enslaved people**, ultimately reaching Scandinavian trading centers and communities thousands of kilometers from the Islamic lands where the coins had been struck. In Scandinavia, foreign coins were often valued primarily for their silver rather than their issuing authority and could circulate by weight alongside ingots, jewelry, and cut pieces of *hacksilver*. The appearance of Tabaristani coins in these northern finds therefore provides a remarkable illustration of how a small silver coin produced on the southern shore of the Caspian Sea could eventually become part of the developing Viking Age bullion economy.

For this collection, C012 provides a particularly useful counterpart to the earlier **Sasanian drachm of Bahram V (C010)** and the **Umayyad fals (C011)**. C010 represents the original imperial tradition from which the portrait-and-fire-altar design developed; C011 demonstrates the emergence of explicitly Arabic and Islamic provincial coinage; and C012 shows that elsewhere, **Sasanian visual and linguistic traditions continued deep into the Islamic period**. Rather than a clean break between “Sasanian” and “Islamic” coinage, these three coins illustrate how political change, language, religion, and visual culture could develop at very different rates.
