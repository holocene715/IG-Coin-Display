This copper **fals** was struck at **Bukhara**, one of the great urban centers of medieval Central Asia, during the period of Samanid rule. Unlike the portraiture and religious imagery found on many earlier coins in this collection, its design is almost entirely epigraphic. Arabic inscriptions fill both faces of the coin, turning religious declarations, the name of the issuing authority, and information about the coin's production into the design itself.

The center of the obverse carries the Islamic profession of divine unity:

**لا إله إلا الله وحده لا شريك له**

*There is no god but God alone; He has no partner.*

Around it appears the mint-and-date formula:

**بسم الله ضرب هذا الفلس ببخارا سنة اربع و أربعين و ثلثمائة**

*In the name of God, this fals was struck at Bukhara in the year four and forty and three hundred.*


The reverse continues the religious and political inscriptions. Its central legend reads:

**لله محمد رسول الله نوح بن منصور**

*For God. Muhammad is the Messenger of God. Nuh ibn Mansur.*

The surrounding inscription reads:

**لله الأمر من قبل و من بعد و يومئذ يفرح المؤمنون بنصر الله**

*To God belongs the command before and after, and on that day the believers will rejoice in the victory of God.*



## Historical Significance

The Samanid Empire was one of the most important states of the eastern Islamic world during the ninth and tenth centuries. From centers including Bukhara and Samarkand, the Samanids ruled much of Transoxiana and Khurasan, encompassing territories in modern Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, Afghanistan, Iran, and neighboring regions. The city of Bukhara flourished under Samanid rule as a center of government, commerce, religion, and scholarship. Its position in Transoxiana placed it within extensive trade networks connecting Iran and the Middle East with the Eurasian steppe, the cities of Central Asia, and routes leading toward China. Samanid coinage consequently circulated within an economy that connected regions far beyond the dynasty's immediate boundaries.
The appearance of C013 is particularly significant when compared with C012, the Abbasid-period hemidrachm of Tabaristan. C012 was struck in 784–785 CE under Muslim rule but still employed a crowned Sasanian-style portrait, a Zoroastrian fire altar, Middle Persian language, and Pahlavi script. By the tenth century, the visual language represented by C013 is dramatically different. Pahlavi has given way to Arabic script, and religious inscriptions have become the dominant elements of the coin's design.
This change should not be misinterpreted as the replacement of Iranian culture by Arab culture. The Samanids were a Persianate Iranian dynasty, and their period became closely associated with the flourishing of New Persian literature and culture. Arabic remained the principal language of Islamic religious expression and appeared extensively on their coinage, while Persian increasingly flourished as a literary and administrative language. C013 therefore represents a society in which Iranian political and cultural traditions had become deeply integrated into the wider Islamic world.
The coin also illustrates the different economic roles played by Islamic denominations. Whereas Samanid silver dirhams became important instruments of long-distance trade and have been discovered in enormous numbers across eastern and northern Europe, copper fals like C013 were primarily local and regional currency, suited to everyday transactions within cities and markets. Within this collection, C013 marks an important stage in the long transformation visible across the preceding coins. Earlier issues show Greek traditions spreading eastward, Iranian rulers adapting Hellenistic imagery, and Islamic governments continuing Sasanian forms. Here, several centuries of cultural change have produced something visually quite different: a Central Asian coin issued by an Iranian dynasty, inscribed entirely in Arabic, and expressing political and religious identity primarily through writing.
