This large copper-alloy **follis** was issued by the **Byzantine Empire** during the reign of **Romanos III Argyros (1028–1034 CE)**. It belongs to the **Anonymous Class B** series, a distinctive group of Byzantine coins that prominently featured Christian religious imagery while omitting the name and portrait of the reigning emperor. The coin was struck at **Constantinople**, the capital of the Byzantine Empire and one of the largest and most important cities of the medieval world. It is among the largest copper denominations used in everyday commercial transactions throughout the empire. Although this coin is traditionally attributed to the reign of Romanos III, his name and portrait do not appear on it. Instead, the design expresses the idea that Christ was the ultimate source of legitimate authority, while the emperor governed as his earthly representative.

Earlier Byzantine coins had generally emphasized the emperor's portrait and titles, reinforcing the personal authority of the ruler. The anonymous folles instead placed **Christ at the center of the coin's imagery**, omitting the emperor's name altogether. This change was particularly significant because copper folles were used in ordinary transactions. Unlike gold coins associated with large payments and state finance, these copper issues circulated widely among merchants, craftspeople, farmers, and urban residents. The image of Christ therefore became a familiar element of everyday economic life throughout the empire. These designs reflect the central role of **Orthodox Christianity** in Byzantine society and the belief that imperial government operated within a divinely ordained political order.

## Historical Significance

The **Byzantine Empire**, also known as the Eastern Roman Empire, preserved the political and institutional traditions of the Roman state long after the collapse of imperial authority in western Europe. Its inhabitants generally understood themselves as **Romans**, while Greek had become the dominant language of government, religion, and everyday life. 

By the early 11th  century, the empire had experienced a period of significant territorial recovery and political consolidation under the **Macedonian dynasty**. During the reign of **Basil II (976–1025 CE)**, Byzantine armies reconquered extensive territories in the Balkans and strengthened imperial control over parts of the eastern Mediterranean and Anatolia. Romanos III Argyros came to the throne in **1028 CE** through his marriage to *Zoe Porphyrogenita*, a daughter of Emperor Constantine VIII. Romanos belonged to the Constantinopolitan aristocracy and had previously served as a senior imperial official.

His reign was marked by ambitious but often unsuccessful attempts to strengthen the empire's military and economic position. In **1030 CE**, Romanos personally led an expedition against the Mirdasid rulers of Aleppo but suffered a significant defeat near Azaz, illustrating the continuing military challenges along the empire's eastern frontier. Despite these difficulties, Constantinople remained a major center of trade, administration, and artistic production. The city's wealth and political importance were supported by extensive commercial networks connecting the Black Sea, Mediterranean, Balkans, and Middle East.

During Romanos III's reign, the Byzantine Empire still controlled much of **Anatolia**, which formed the economic and military heartland of the state. This region contained important agricultural lands, fortified cities, trade routes, and military recruitment centers. Over the following decades, however, Byzantine authority in Anatolia came under increasing pressure from Turkic migration and Seljuk expansion. The decisive Byzantine defeat at the **Battle of Manzikert in 1071 CE** accelerated the fragmentation of imperial control across much of the Anatolian interior. This transformation eventually contributed to the emergence of the **Seljuk Sultanate of Rûm** and other regional states represented in the collection's **Medieval Anatolia** series.
