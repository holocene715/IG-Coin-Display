Struck in Rome in **1866**, this copper **one soldo** was issued under **Pope Pius IX**, during the final years of the Papal States as an independent territorial state. Its obverse depicts the pope in profile, while the reverse identifies the issuing government and gives the denomination as both one soldo and five centesimi. The coin reflects the modernization of papal currency at a time when Italian unification had reduced the pope's once-extensive territories to Rome and the surrounding region.

## Historical Background

For more than a thousand years, the **Papal States** formed a distinctive political entity governed by the popes in their dual capacities as spiritual leaders of the Catholic Church and temporal sovereigns. During the Renaissance, papal authority extended across substantial territories in central Italy, including Rome, Umbria, the Marches, and parts of Emilia-Romagna. The collection's **mezzo giulio of Pope Clement VII (C028)**, struck between 1523 and 1534, represents this period of extensive papal political influence, when the pope participated directly in the military and diplomatic struggles of Renaissance Europe. The **douzain of Pope Clement VIII (C031)**, issued later in the sixteenth century in the papal territories around Avignon, demonstrates another dimension of papal sovereignty: the Church governed territories beyond the Italian peninsula, including the **Comtat Venaissin**, an enclave surrounded by French lands. Together, these earlier coins illustrate the geographical reach and political complexity of the Renaissance papacy. By contrast, when **Pius IX issued the one soldo of 1866 (C047)**, most of these territories had been lost, leaving the pope in control of little more than Rome and its surrounding region.

By the nineteenth century, the political position of the Papal States had changed dramatically. The **French Revolution and Napoleonic Wars** challenged traditional monarchies throughout Europe, and French forces occupied and reorganized papal territories on several occasions. Although the **Congress of Vienna in 1815** restored much of the Papal States to papal rule, the restoration proved increasingly difficult to maintain. Liberal and nationalist movements sought constitutional government and the unification of the Italian peninsula, while the papal administration resisted reforms that threatened its temporal sovereignty. **Pius IX**, elected pope in 1846, initially encouraged expectations of political reform, but the revolutionary upheavals of 1848–1849 and the establishment of a short-lived Roman Republic hardened his opposition to Italian nationalism. French military intervention restored papal authority in Rome, leaving the survival of the state increasingly dependent upon foreign support.

The decisive territorial losses came during the **Risorgimento**, the movement for Italian unification. In **1860**, forces associated with the unification movement conquered the papal provinces of the Marches and Umbria, including territories that had been governed from cities such as Ancona and Perugia. The **Kingdom of Italy** was proclaimed in 1861, with Victor Emmanuel II as king, but Rome remained outside the new kingdom. By the time this soldo was minted in **1866**, the Papal States consisted principally of Rome and the surrounding territory of Lazio, a fraction of the central Italian lands ruled by Renaissance popes. The contrast with **C028** is therefore particularly striking: both coins were issued by popes in Rome, but the political geography and international standing of their state had been fundamentally transformed.

The coin also illustrates the **modernization of papal currency** during these final years. In 1866, the Papal States adopted a decimal monetary system based on the **papal lira**, replacing the older system of scudi, baiocchi, and quattrini. The new currency was designed to align more closely with the monetary standards being adopted elsewhere in Europe, including those of the recently unified Kingdom of Italy. This soldo carried a value of **five centesimi**, or one-twentieth of a lira, clearly expressed on the reverse alongside the traditional denomination. The inscription *STATO PONTIFICIO* asserted the continued existence of the papal state, while the portrait of Pius IX emphasized the personal sovereignty of the pope. Its relatively modern design, decimal denomination, and standardized copper composition distinguish it from the silver and billon coinages of earlier papal centuries.

The final collapse of papal territorial sovereignty came in **September 1870**, when Italian troops entered Rome following the withdrawal of the French garrison during the Franco-Prussian War. The capture of the city ended the Papal States as an independent territorial state, and Rome was subsequently incorporated into the Kingdom of Italy, becoming its capital in 1871. Pius IX rejected the legitimacy of the annexation and withdrew into the Vatican, beginning the prolonged dispute between the papacy and the Italian government known as the **Roman Question**. That dispute was not formally resolved until the **Lateran Treaty of 1929**, which established the independent Vatican City State. Issued just four years before the loss of Rome, this **1866 soldo** is among the final coins of the Papal States and provides a compelling conclusion to the collection's portrayal of the papacy as both a religious institution and a territorial power.
