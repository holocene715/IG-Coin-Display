This silver **tram** was issued during the reign of **Leo I (Levon I)**, the first crowned king of the medieval **Kingdom of Cilician Armenia**, which occupied the coastal plains and mountainous interior of southeastern Anatolia. Leo ruled as king from **1198 to 1219 CE**, overseeing a period of political consolidation, commercial expansion, and increasing contact with the Christian kingdoms of Europe.

The obverse depicts **Leo seated on a royal throne**, wearing a crown and mantle. He holds a cross in one hand and a **fleur-de-lis** in the other, while his feet rest upon a footstool. The throne is decorated with lions, reinforcing the royal character of the image. An Armenian inscription surrounding the portrait identifies him as **"Levon, King of the Armenians."** The reverse features a tall **double-barred cross flanked by two lions**, with both animals facing inward toward the central symbol. The surrounding Armenian inscription translates as **"By the will of God."** Together, the inscriptions and imagery emphasize Leo's royal authority, his Christian faith, and the religious legitimacy of his kingship.

This coin is attributed here to the **mint of Sis**, the principal royal capital of Cilician Armenia, located at modern **Kozan in southern Turkey**. Sis became a major political and monetary center of the Armenian kingdom.


## Historical Significance

The **Kingdom of Cilician Armenia** emerged from the displacement and migration of Armenian communities following the Byzantine and Seljuk struggles for control of eastern Anatolia during the 12th century. As Armenian nobles established themselves in the mountains and plains of Cilicia, a new Armenian political center gradually developed along the northeastern Mediterranean coast, geographically separated from the traditional Armenian highlands farther east. The kingdom's position was particularly important because Cilicia occupied a strategic corridor between **Anatolia, Syria, Mesopotamia, and the Mediterranean Sea**. Mountain passes through the Taurus and Amanus ranges connected its fortified towns and agricultural valleys to inland trade routes. Coastal ports provided access to Mediterranean commerce, allowing Cilician Armenia to develop strong economic and diplomatic relationships with Italian maritime republics and other western powers.

**Leo I** was one of the most consequential rulers in the kingdom's history. Before his coronation, he ruled as prince of Cilicia and worked to consolidate the territories controlled by the Armenian nobility. His coronation in **1198 or 1199 CE** elevated Cilician Armenia from a principality to a recognized kingdom and strengthened its standing among neighboring Christian and Muslim powers. Leo's kingship developed in the wider context of the **Crusades**, during which western European states established territories along the eastern Mediterranean coast. Cilician Armenia maintained particularly close, though sometimes contentious, relationships with the Crusader states of Antioch, Tripoli, and Jerusalem. Armenian rulers participated in regional alliances, dynastic marriages, military campaigns, and diplomatic negotiations involving both Christian and Muslim neighbors.

The coin's **royal portrait and fleur-de-lis** reflect the kingdom's participation in the broader visual language of medieval Christian monarchy. The fleur-de-lis was widely used as a royal and heraldic emblem in western Europe, while the enthroned ruler was a familiar symbol of authority in Byzantine and European artistic traditions. These influences were adapted into a distinctly Armenian form through the use of Armenian inscriptions and local iconographic conventions. The **two lions on the reverse** are particularly significant. Lions were longstanding symbols of power, courage, and kingship throughout the Mediterranean and Near East. In Cilician Armenian coinage, the lion became an especially prominent royal emblem. The arrangement of two lions flanking a Christian cross combines this imagery of earthly royal power with the religious foundation of the monarchy.

The **Armenian language and alphabet** distinguish this coin from many of the surrounding medieval monetary traditions. Developed in the early fifth century and traditionally attributed to Mesrop Mashtots, the Armenian alphabet helped preserve a distinctive literary, religious, and cultural identity. Its use on royal coinage reinforced the legitimacy of Armenian kingship while communicating that identity through the everyday medium of currency.

The kingdom survived long after Leo's reign, but its political circumstances changed dramatically during the thirteenth century. As the **Mongol Empire** expanded westward, Cilician Armenian rulers established alliances with the Mongols, hoping to secure protection against neighboring Muslim powers. These relationships connected the kingdom to the wider political transformations that reshaped Central Asia and the Middle East. Cilician Armenia ultimately came under increasing pressure from the **Mamluk Sultanate**, which gradually reduced its territory and military strength. The kingdom's capital at Sis fell to the Mamluks in **1375 CE**, ending the independent Armenian monarchy in Cilicia.
