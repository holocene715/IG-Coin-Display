This bronze **fals** was struck at **Ilaq**, a district southeast of present-day Tashkent, in **AH 388 (998 CE)**, during the expansion of Qarakhanid authority across Transoxiana. Its Arabic inscriptions identify **Mansur ibn Ahmad**, a local ruler described by the title *dihqān*, and refer to higher-ranking Qarakhanid authorities. Unlike a coin that names only a single sovereign, this issue offers a glimpse of the layered political relationships governing a Central Asian district at the end of the tenth century.

The obverse places the phrase **دهقان الجليل** (*dihqān al-jalīl*, "the glorious dihqān") at its center. Around it is the Islamic declaration of faith, and the outer inscription records that the fals was struck at **Ilaq in AH 388**. The reverse names **Tegin Aba Salih**, identified in the Leu catalog as Nasr ibn Ali, while the surrounding legend invokes **Ahmad ibn Ali**, asking God to support him. The arrangement combines a local title, religious declarations, a mint and date, and the names of rulers with different degrees of authority.

The title *dihqān* has roots in the social structure of pre-Islamic Iran and Central Asia. It traditionally referred to members of the landed local elite and came to be associated with district-level authority. Its appearance on this coin is particularly revealing: even as a Turkic ruling dynasty established power over the region, older Iranian administrative and social terminology remained meaningful.

The inscription transcriptions above follow the published Leu catalog and a related entry in the Qarakhanid Oriental Coins Database. They are presented in normalized Arabic spelling rather than as a letter-for-letter facsimile of the engraved Kufic script. The cited Leu specimen weighs **2.99 g** and measures **27 mm**; these measurements should be replaced if the C016 specimen differs.

## Historical Significance

The **Qarakhanids** were a Turkic ruling dynasty that rose to power in Central Asia during the tenth century. Their conversion to Islam and expansion into the cities of Transoxiana marked a major political transition: territories long associated with the Iranian **Samanid dynasty** increasingly came under Qarakhanid control. In **999 CE**, just one year after the date on this coin, Qarakhanid forces captured Bukhara, bringing Samanid rule in Transoxiana to an end. The change of dynasty, however, did not mean an immediate break with the region's established urban economy, Islamic institutions, or Persianate administrative traditions.

**Ilaq**, in the wider Tashkent region, was part of an economically important landscape of settlements, agriculture, trade routes, and mineral resources. Copper fals such as C016 served as local or regional small-denomination currency, in contrast to the silver dirhams more closely associated with long-distance exchange. The coin's mint-and-date inscription demonstrates the continuing importance of named regional mints in an interconnected Central Asian monetary system.

The coin is especially informative because its inscriptions reflect a **hierarchy of power**. Mansur ibn Ahmad is identified through the local title *dihqān*, while Tegin Aba Salih and Ahmad ibn Ali represent higher levels of Qarakhanid authority. This combination suggests that governance could incorporate local elites and existing institutions within a wider dynastic framework. The title *dihqān* is particularly significant for the collection's theme of cultural exchange: it is an Iranian term appearing in Arabic script on a coin issued under Turkic rule.

Placed after the Samanid fals of **C014 and C015**, C016 illustrates both continuity and change around the turn of the millennium. The coin preserves the established Islamic practice of using Arabic inscriptions rather than royal portraits, while its named authorities reveal a new political order. Its mixture of Turkic dynastic power, Iranian local terminology, and Arabic religious and monetary language provides a compact record of the overlapping cultures that shaped medieval Central Asia.
