This silver drachm was issued during the reign of **Antiochos VII Euergetes**, also known as Antiochos VII Sidetes. Antiochus VII was one of the last Seleucid kings *(rulers succeeding Seleucus, one of Alexander the Great’s powerful generals who seized control after Alexander’s death)* to exercise substantial power beyond modern-day Syria. 

The coin’s obverse depicts the king wearing the royal diadem, while the reverse shows Nike, the Greek personification of victory, advancing left and carrying a wreath. The Greek inscription identifies the ruler as King Antiochos Euergetes, using an epithet meaning *“Benefactor.”*

## Historical Significance

**Antiochos VII Sidetes** presided over one of the last major attempts to restore the territorial power of the Seleucid Empire. The Seleucid Empire was a vast kingdom founded by one of Alexander the Great’s generals after Alexander’s death in 323 BCE. At its height, it stretched from the eastern Mediterranean into Central Asia, bringing Greek traditions into contact with many older cultures. Its territory gradually shrank as rival powers rose, and Rome eventually took control of its remaining lands in 64 BCE. However, after taking the throne during a period of dynastic instability, Antiochus VII campaigned successfully in the eastern Mediterranean and launched a major expedition eastward against the **Parthian Empire** (see coins C006 and C007). His forces temporarily recovered substantial portions of Mesopotamia and territories farther east that had previously been lost by the Seleucids.

The territorial restoration Antiochus brought was short-lived. He was killed during renewed fighting with the Parthians in **129 BCE**, after which Seleucid power and influence contracted further toward Syria. Antiochus’s death marks a turning point at which the Seleucids gave in to the ongoing push from the expanding Parthian force in the region. Later coins in this display start to show increasing expression of Persian and central Asian language and culture.
