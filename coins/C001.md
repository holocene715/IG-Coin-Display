This small copper coin, the earliest chronologically in this series, was struck at **Pergamum** (also called Pergamon), during the reign of **Attalos II Philadelphos**. Pergamum was the capital of the Attalid Kingdom, a Hellenistic (Greek) state in the 3rd to 2nd centuries BC that made up much of the western half of modern-day Turkey. Pergamum was a very wealthy and influential city in the region and exhibited a mix of Greek and Persian cultures. 

The coin's design is decidedly Greek. The obverse, or front of the coin, depicts **Athena** wearing a helmet, while the reverse shows a coiled serpent with its head raised. The Greek inscription reads **ΦΙΛΕΤΑΙΡΟΥ**, “of Philetairos,” referring to *Philetairos*, founder of the Attalid dynasty. The continued use of his name by later Attalid rulers, even more than 200 years after his death, linked their coinage to the dynasty’s founder and reinforced the legitimacy and continuity of Attalid rule.

## Historical Significance

**Attalos II Philadelphos** was the fifth of seven Attalid dynasty rulers and ruled Pergamon in the decades leading up to the dynasty’s fall. He was a successful military leader for his elder brother and king, Eumenes II, prior to taking the thrown himself in 159 BC. He was frequently sent by his brother to Rome as an envoy, and the Romans even offered to assist him in a coup against Eumenes II. This was unnecessary however, as his brother died without a viable heir, leaving Attalos an opening to take power. He ruled for nearly 20 years, into his early 80s.

The Kingdom of Pergamon was one of many states that came about after the death of Alexander the Great in 323 BC. When Alexander was killed, the massive territory he had rapidly conquered between Macedonia and India was seized and fractured among his competing generals. One of these generals and Alexander’s personal bodyguard, Lysimachus, seized control of the territory that would eventually become Pergamon. Lysimachus’s own lieutenant, Philetaerus, took advantage of a plot to kill Lysimachus and rose to power in as the founder of the Attalid dynasty in 281 BC. 
The kingdom was also a major center of *Hellenistic culture and scholarship*. Pergamum's architecture, sanctuary complexes, sculpture, and famous library made the city one of the leading cultural centers of the Greek-speaking world, which extended far east and south of modern-day Greece. In 133 BC, a few decades after this coin was issued, Attalus’s son and the last Attalid king, **Attalos III**, bequeathed his kingdom to Rome. Much of the territory subsequently became the Roman province of *Asia*, making the Attalid kingdom an important link between the Hellenistic political world and the expanding Roman presence in the eastern Mediterranean.
