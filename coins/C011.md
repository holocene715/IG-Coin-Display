This anonymous bronze fals belongs to the provincial copper coinage of the **Umayyad Caliphate** and is generally attributed to **Palestine**, although its precise mint and date remain uncertain. Unlike the increasingly standardized precious-metal coinage of the Umayyad state, provincial bronze issues could retain a surprising variety of images and designs.

The most distinctive feature of this type is the **bird flying left**, conventionally identified as a dove. Around it appears the declaration *Muhammad rasul Allah* (**محمد رسول الله**), meaning “Muhammad is the Messenger of God.” The opposite side carries the profession of God's unity, *la ilaha illa Allah wahdahu la sharika lahu* (**لا إله إلا الله وحده لا شريك له**), meaning “There is no god but God alone; He has no partner.”

The coin is cataloged as **Album 164** and belongs to a broader group of early Islamic pictorial bronze coins associated with Palestine. Its combination of explicitly Islamic Arabic inscriptions with a prominent animal image makes it an especially interesting example of the diversity that persisted in provincial copper coinage during the early Islamic period.

## Historical Significance

The **Umayyad Caliphate** ruled from 661 to 750 CE and governed an enormous territory extending from the Iberian Peninsula and North Africa through the Middle East and into Central and South Asia. Its capital at Damascus placed Syria and Palestine near the political center of the empire.

During the reign of **ʿAbd al-Malik** (685–705 CE), the Umayyad state undertook major monetary and administrative reforms. Earlier Islamic coinage had drawn heavily upon the Byzantine and Sasanian monetary systems inherited in the conquered territories. By the end of the seventh century, new gold and silver coinage instead emphasized Arabic inscriptions and explicitly Islamic declarations. This fals belongs to the monetary world that developed after those reforms, but provincial bronze coinage remained substantially more varied than the standardized gold dinars and silver dirhams. Local copper issues in Syria and Palestine could retain distinctive symbols and pictorial elements even as Arabic became the dominant language of the coins. The combination seen here—a bird together with *Muhammad rasul Allah* (**محمد رسول الله**) and the declaration of divine unity, *la ilaha illa Allah wahdahu la sharika lahu* (**لا إله إلا الله وحده لا شريك له**)—is a particularly striking example of that coexistence. The **flying dove** therefore makes this coin useful for illustrating the gradual nature of the transition from Late Antique to Islamic visual culture. The inscriptions proclaim an unmistakably Islamic religious identity in Arabic, while the coin still employs a prominent pictorial symbol. Rather than representing an instantaneous change from figural imagery to purely inscriptional currency, the type demonstrates the continued diversity of everyday regional coinage during the early Islamic period.

C011 also forms a particularly strong transition from the preceding Sasanian drachm in this collection. C010 represents the mature monetary tradition of the **Sasanian Empire**, while C011 belongs to the new political order created by the early Islamic conquests, which brought the former Sasanian Empire and large portions of the Byzantine Near East under Muslim rule. The Arabic inscriptions on this coin consequently mark a major change in the collection's visual and linguistic history. Earlier coins demonstrate the long survival and transformation of **Greek and Iranian numismatic traditions**; here Arabic becomes both the language and one of the principal visual elements of the coin itself.
