This billon tetradrachm was issued by **Aspavarma**, a ruler or military governor associated with the **Apracharaja dynasty** of ancient Gandhara. His coinage belongs to the complex political landscape of the northwestern Indian subcontinent during the early first century CE, when Indo-Scythian, Indo-Parthian, and local dynasties competed for control of the region.

The obverse depicts a ruler on horseback, continuing a mounted royal type that had become prominent on earlier **Indo-Scythian coinage**. The surrounding Greek inscription is derived from the royal titulature used on the coins of **Azes**, although on Aspavarma's issues the Greek had become increasingly corrupted and was no longer necessarily intended as a fully intelligible text. The reverse depicts **Pallas Athena standing right**, preserving another unmistakably Greek element of the region's numismatic tradition. 

The reverse inscription is written in **Gandhari using Kharosthi script** and identifies Aspavarma as the son of Indravarma and as a victorious *strategos*, or general. The combination of Greek-derived imagery and titulature with a locally meaningful Kharosthi inscription makes the coin a particularly clear example of the cultural layering characteristic of Gandharan coinage. 

## Historical Significance

Aspavarma ruled in **Gandhara during the first century CE**, a period when the region stood at the intersection of Iranian, Central Asian, Indian, and surviving Hellenistic traditions. His coins belong to the Apracharajas, a dynasty associated particularly with the northwestern regions around Gandhara and Bajaur. Numismatic and inscriptional evidence identifies Aspavarma as the son of **Indravarma**, and his coinage appears shortly before the expansion of Kushan power into the region. 

For this collection, the coin provides an especially strong example of **lasting Greek influence**. Roughly three centuries after Alexander the Great's campaigns in the region, Aspavarma's currency still employed Greek-derived royal imagery, the mounted-king format inherited from Indo-Scythian predecessors, and the figure of Athena. Yet these elements existed alongside Gandhari language, Kharosthi writing, Buddhist-associated symbols, and an indigenous dynastic identity. The result is not simply a survival of Greek culture, but a distinctly Gandharan coinage in which Hellenistic traditions had become part of a much broader regional visual vocabulary.
