This coin is a silver drachm, an intermediate denomination of Greek coinage issued during the reign of **Heliocles I**, one of the final Greek rulers to exercise authority over the region known as Bactria. The Greco-Bactrian Kingdom emerged in the 3rd century BC when the Seleucid province of Bactria, in what is now Afghanistan and Central Asia, became independent following the collapse of Alexander the Great’s conquests. Bactria includes parts of modern-day Afghanistan, Tajikistan, and Uzbekistan. Its rulers combined Greek traditions with influences from neighboring cultures and expanded into parts of the Indian subcontinent. The Greco-Bactrian kingdom fragmented in the 2nd century BC, but its art and coinage preserve a vivid record of this meeting of cultures.

The obverse depicts Heliocles wearing a **Boeotian-style helmet and royal diadem**, while the reverse shows **Zeus enthroned**, holding a small winged figure of **Nike**, the Greek personification of victory. The entirely Greek inscription identifies the ruler as **King Heliocles Dikaios**, or “Heliocles the Just.” The strongly Hellenistic imagery is particularly striking given that the coin was produced deep within Central Asia, more than a 150 years after the campaigns of Alexander the Great.


## Historical Significance

**Heliocles I** ruled during the final phase of Greek political control over Bactria itself. The Greco-Bactrian Kingdom had developed from the eastern territories Seleucus, one of Alexander the Great’s most powerful and driven generals. Bactria eventually split from the Seleucid Empire (see C003-C005) into an independent Hellenistic state in Central Asia. By the reign of Heliocles in the mid-2nd century BC, however, Bactria was coming under increasing economic and military pressure from the *Yuezhi peoples* migrating from the north and east. Related Greek-ruled territories south of the Hindu Kush mountains continued to develop into the Indo-Greek kingdoms (see C008) as Greco-Bactria faded.

Heliocles is linked to an important transition in the history of Central Asia. He is generally regarded as the **last major Greek king to rule Bactria north of the Hindu Kush**, after which Greek political power in the region contracted southward and Bactria increasingly came under the control of Central Asian groups that ultimately preceded the rise of the **Kushan Empire** (see C010). The Greek language, royal portraiture, and imagery of Zeus and Nike on this coin provide especially clear evidence of how enduring those cultural traditions had become. The invading Yuezhi peoples, despite being decidedly non-Greek, continued to issue coinage with the design and likeness of Heliocles I for many years after his death in 120 BC.
