This small silver 2-soldi coin was issued by the Republic of Venice for circulation in the Levant, the eastern Mediterranean region with which Venetian merchants maintained extensive commercial relationships. The type was authorized by decree on September 12, 1565, during the dogeship of Girolamo Priuli (1559–1567), and continued to be produced under later doges until approximately 1600. Because the coins are undated, an individual specimen cannot necessarily be assigned to Priuli's reign. The year 1565 marks the introduction of the type, rather than a confirmed date of striking for this specimen.
The obverse bears the winged Lion of Saint Mark, the emblem of Venice, with a paw resting upon the Gospels. The surrounding inscription, SANCTVS MARCVS VENET, identifies the city's patron saint. The reverse depicts Saint Justina holding a sword and scales, flanked by lions. Its inscription, DILIGITE IVSTITIAM, means “Love justice.” The reference type is approximately 17 mm across and weighs about 0.9 g, although the present specimen has not yet been measured.
Historical Significance
Venice and the Eastern Mediterranean
By the sixteenth century, the Republic of Venice had been a leading Mediterranean maritime and commercial power for centuries. Its merchants connected the markets of northern Italy and central Europe with ports across the Adriatic, Aegean, and eastern Mediterranean. Goods moving through these networks included spices, textiles, silk, metals, and other valuable commodities.
Venice governed not only the lagoon city itself but also mainland territories in northern Italy and an extensive, geographically scattered collection of overseas possessions known as the Stato da Mar. In 1565 these included Crete, Cyprus, Corfu, and parts of Dalmatia, alongside other islands, ports, and coastal strongholds. The republic's commercial influence reached considerably farther than the territory it directly administered.
The eastern Mediterranean was also an arena of intense competition with the Ottoman Empire. Venice sought to preserve access to eastern markets even as Ottoman expansion challenged its military and territorial position. Trade and diplomacy therefore continued alongside periodic warfare.
A Coin Intended for the Levant
This 2-soldi issue is particularly revealing because it was specifically intended for circulation in the Levant. Its existence reflects the need for currency that could function within commercial networks spanning multiple political jurisdictions and monetary traditions.
A soldo was a unit within the Venetian lira system, with twenty soldi conventionally accounting for one lira. The 2-soldi denomination represented a comparatively small payment, unlike the high-value gold ducats for which Venice was also famous. Together, Venice's gold and silver issues served different needs within its extensive economy.
The type's authorization in 1565 and continued production into the following decades show that the Venetian state maintained a specialized monetary presence in its eastern trading sphere. The precise circumstances of an individual coin's circulation, however, cannot be established from its design alone.
Saint Mark and the Venetian Republic
The winged Lion of Saint Mark was among the most recognizable symbols of Venice. According to Venetian tradition, the relics of Saint Mark the Evangelist had been brought to the city from Alexandria in 828 CE. The saint became Venice's patron and a central emblem of its civic and political identity.
The lion appears on Venetian flags, public buildings, official seals, and coins. Its presence on this small silver issue asserted the authority of the republic even far from Venice itself. The book beneath its paw represents the Gospels associated with the evangelist.
This imagery also provides continuity with C027, the silver grosso of Doge Giovanni Soranzo (1312–1328). Although the two coins were issued more than two centuries apart, both employ the authority and imagery of Saint Mark to identify the Venetian state.
Saint Justina and the Ideal of Justice
The reverse depicts Saint Justina, traditionally associated with Padua and venerated in Venetian territories. She holds two attributes associated with the administration and defense of justice: a sword and scales. The legend DILIGITE IVSTITIAM—“Love justice”—reinforces the message.
The inscription evokes the biblical exhortation in the Book of Wisdom to love justice, and expresses a moral ideal appropriate to a government that presented itself as orderly, lawful, and divinely protected. It also illustrates how Venice combined religious figures with broader civic virtues in its coin designs.
The presence of lions beside Saint Justina further connects the image to the republic's established visual language. Together, the two sides communicate both Venetian political identity and an ideal of just government.
Venice at a Turning Point
The date 1565 places the introduction of this coin shortly before a major change in Venetian power in the eastern Mediterranean. In 1570, the Ottoman Empire invaded Cyprus, then under Venetian rule. The ensuing war culminated in the famous Battle of Lepanto in 1571, in which a coalition including Venice defeated an Ottoman fleet. Despite that naval victory, Venice relinquished Cyprus under the peace settlement of 1573.
The 2-soldi type thus began circulating at a moment when Venice still held Cyprus but faced growing Ottoman pressure. Its continued production into later decades crossed a period of major territorial and diplomatic change.
A Small Coin from a Far-Reaching Republic
C030 demonstrates that the history of Mediterranean trade is not represented only by large gold coins or prestigious silver denominations. Smaller coins also played an important role in the everyday monetary systems connecting ports, merchants, and communities.
Together with C027, this coin traces the evolution of Venetian money from the medieval grosso to the more specialized issues of the sixteenth century. The recurring imagery of Saint Mark underscores the remarkable continuity of the Venetian Republic, while the Levantine purpose of C030 highlights the republic's enduring dependence on international trade.
