This bronze **tetrachalkon** was issued by the city of **Amisos**, on the southern coast of the Black Sea, during the reign of **Mithridates VI Eupator**, king of Pontus. Dated to approximately **85–65 BCE**, the coin belongs to a period when Pontus was deeply involved in conflict with the expanding Roman Republic. Although struck under a powerful monarch, it bears the name of the city rather than a royal portrait, reflecting the continued importance of Greek civic identity within the Pontic kingdom.

The obverse depicts a **Gorgoneion**, the frontal face of the mythological Gorgon Medusa, at the center of an **aegis**. In Greek visual tradition, the Gorgoneion was an *apotropaic* image: its frightening appearance was thought to ward off danger and protect its bearer. The aegis, commonly associated with Athena and Zeus, reinforces this protective symbolism.

The reverse shows **Nike**, the Greek personification of victory, advancing to the right with a wreath and palm branch, both recognizable emblems of triumph. The Greek inscription **ΑΜΙΣΟΥ** (*Amisou*) means **“of Amisos,”** identifying the city responsible for the issue. Together, the protective Gorgoneion and victorious Nike create a compact visual statement of strength, divine favor, and success.

## Historical Significance

The **Kingdom of Pontus** emerged as a major Hellenistic power in northern Anatolia, combining Greek urban culture with political and dynastic traditions rooted in the wider Iranian and Anatolian worlds. Its ruling Mithridatid dynasty claimed connections to the Persian nobility, while its cities—including Amisos—participated in the Greek-speaking commercial and cultural networks of the Black Sea. The coinage of Pontus reflects this mixture: Greek language and mythology appear on money issued within a kingdom whose royal identity drew on more than one cultural tradition.

Under **Mithridates VI Eupator (120–63 BCE)**, Pontus expanded its influence around the Black Sea and across parts of Anatolia. Mithridates became one of Rome's most persistent adversaries, fighting a series of conflicts now known as the **Mithridatic Wars**. The dating of this coin, **85–65 BCE**, places it within the decades of these struggles, when military campaigns, shifting alliances, and competition over Anatolian cities reshaped the eastern Mediterranean. Its imagery of protection and victory would have been readily understood in this turbulent setting, although it should not be read as commemorating a specific battle without additional evidence.

**Amisos**, the city named on the reverse, was an important port linking inland Anatolia to maritime exchange around the Black Sea. The inscription **ΑΜΙΣΟΥ** demonstrates the persistence of local civic identities even as the city operated within a larger monarchy. Bronze denominations such as this tetrachalkon were suited to everyday regional transactions, in contrast to the precious-metal coinage more readily associated with large payments and long-distance transfers.

Within this collection, C006 broadens the story of the **Hellenistic world** beyond the Seleucid and Greco-Bactrian kingdoms. Its Greek inscription and familiar mythological figures show how widely shared artistic and religious conventions could be adapted by communities across different political and cultural settings. It also introduces a turning point in the region's history: the collision between the Hellenistic kingdoms of Anatolia and the expanding power of Rome, whose eventual victory over Mithridates transformed the political geography of the eastern Mediterranean.
