Struck at the **Royal Mint in London in 1937**, this bronze penny was issued during the first full calendar year of the reign of **King George VI**, who unexpectedly inherited the British throne following the abdication of his elder brother, Edward VIII. Its obverse bears an uncrowned portrait of the new king, while the reverse retains the traditional seated figure of **Britannia**, a symbol of British identity and maritime power. Minted on the eve of the Second World War, the coin represents a monarchy navigating profound political change, as the British Empire confronted growing demands for independence and an increasingly unstable international order.

## Historical Background

The accession of **King George VI** followed one of the most significant constitutional crises in modern British history. His elder brother, **Edward VIII**, became king in January 1936 following the death of George V. However, Edward's intention to marry **Wallis Simpson**, an American divorcée, created a conflict with the expectations of the monarchy, the Church of England, and the governments of the British Commonwealth. Edward abdicated in December 1936 after less than a year on the throne, leaving his younger brother Albert, Duke of York, to become **George VI**. The new king was crowned at Westminster Abbey on **12 May 1937**, a ceremony that reinforced the continuity of the monarchy following the upheaval of the previous year. Unlike his predecessor, George VI had not been raised with the expectation of becoming king, and his reign would soon be defined by the extraordinary challenges of global war and imperial transformation.

The coin's portrait of George VI was designed by *Thomas Humphrey Paget*, whose initials *HP* appear beneath the king's neck. Unlike the crowned and veiled portrait of **Queen Victoria on the collection's 1898 penny (C048)**, George VI is depicted bareheaded, following a long-standing convention in British coinage that distinguished male sovereigns' portraits from certain ceremonial representations. The Latin inscription identifies him as **King of all the Britons, Defender of the Faith, and Emperor of India**, preserving royal titles that reflected the constitutional and imperial traditions of the British monarchy. The reverse features **Britannia**, seated with a trident and shield, continuing a design tradition that had appeared on British copper coinage since the 17th century. Although the reverse was modified for the new reign, its fundamental imagery remained closely related to the Victorian penny.

The Britain of **1937** differed markedly from the empire represented by Victoria's penny of **1898**. During the late Victorian era, Britain had been among the world's foremost industrial and financial powers, and its empire was expanding across Africa and Asia. By George VI's accession, the **First World War**, the economic disruption of the interwar period, and the **Great Depression** had weakened Britain's relative international position. Although the empire remained extensive, its political structure was changing. The **Statute of Westminster of 1931** had recognized the legislative independence of several self-governing Dominions, including Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and South Africa, subject to differing arrangements for adoption and implementation. Meanwhile, demands for greater self-government were intensifying in India and other colonial territories. The coin's continued use of the title **Emperor of India** reflects an imperial order that still existed in 1937 but would be transformed within a decade by Indian independence in 1947.

The year **1937** also fell within a period of mounting international tension that would soon culminate in the **Second World War**. In Europe, Nazi Germany under Adolf Hitler was rearming and challenging the postwar international settlement, while Fascist Italy pursued expansionist policies in Africa and the Mediterranean. The **Spanish Civil War**, which began in 1936, had become a major ideological and military conflict involving intervention by foreign powers. In Asia, the outbreak of full-scale war between Japan and China in 1937 further destabilized the international order. Britain, still recovering from the economic and human costs of the First World War, sought to preserve peace through diplomacy while gradually expanding its military preparations. When war broke out in September **1939**, George VI became an important symbol of national unity. His decision to remain in London during the Blitz, alongside Queen Elizabeth, strengthened the monarchy's public identification with the hardships experienced by the British population.

The **1937 penny** was produced in substantial numbers, with approximately **88.9 million circulation pieces** struck. Measuring about **31 millimeters** in diameter and weighing **9.45 grams**, it retained the physical characteristics of the large bronze pennies used throughout the Victorian and Edwardian periods. Like the 1898 penny represented by **C048**, it belonged to Britain's pre-decimal monetary system, in which **240 pennies equaled one pound sterling**. These large bronze coins continued circulating until Britain's decimalization in 1971, long after the imperial political order that produced them had begun to disappear. As the final coin in this collection, the **1937 George VI penny** illustrates how coinage can preserve the symbols of political continuity even during periods of extraordinary historical change. Across the collection's nearly two millennia of monetary history, coins document the rise and fall of kingdoms and empires, the spread of religious and cultural traditions, and the changing ways rulers and states expressed their authority. The familiar image of Britannia on this final penny offers a fitting conclusion: an ancient classical symbol carried forward into the modern world, linking the traditions of earlier civilizations with the political transformations of the twentieth century.
