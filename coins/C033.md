This **three-polker coin**, known in Polish as a *półtorak*, was minted in **1624 CE** at **Bydgoszcz** during the reign of **Sigismund III Vasa (1587–1632)**, King of Poland and Grand Duke of Lithuania.

The coin was valued at **1½ grosz**, equivalent to three polker. It was part of a widely circulating series of small silver coins produced in substantial quantities during the early seventeenth century.

The **obverse** features the crowned coat of arms of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, combining the heraldic symbols of Poland and Lithuania with the dynastic arms of the Vasa family. The surrounding Latin inscription identifies Sigismund III and his royal titles.

The **reverse** depicts a cross-topped imperial orb containing the abbreviated date, surrounded by an inscription identifying the coin as new money of the Kingdom of Poland.

The orb design and denomination reflect the influence of Central European monetary traditions, particularly the three-kreuzer coins that circulated widely in neighboring territories.

## Historical Significance

### The Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth

The **Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth** was established in **1569** through the Union of Lublin, which united the Kingdom of Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania into a single political federation.

At its greatest extent, the Commonwealth was among the largest states in Europe, encompassing much of modern Poland, Lithuania, Belarus, Ukraine, and neighboring territories.

Its political system was unusual for the period. Rather than functioning as an absolute monarchy, it operated as an **elective monarchy**, in which the king was chosen by the nobility and governed alongside a powerful parliament known as the *Sejm*.

The Commonwealth was also characterized by considerable religious, linguistic, and cultural diversity.

The combined Polish and Lithuanian heraldry on this coin symbolizes the union of two historically distinct political entities under a shared monarch.

### Sigismund III Vasa

**Sigismund III Vasa** ruled the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth from **1587 to 1632** and was also King of Sweden from 1592 until his deposition in 1599.

A member of the Swedish Vasa dynasty, Sigismund sought to strengthen royal authority and promote Catholicism during a period of intense religious and political competition in Europe.

His reign was marked by prolonged conflicts with Sweden, Muscovy, and the Ottoman Empire, reflecting the Commonwealth's position between several major European powers.

In **1596**, Sigismund transferred the principal royal residence and court from Kraków to Warsaw, contributing to Warsaw's emergence as the political center of the Commonwealth.

By 1624, when this coin was minted, Sigismund had ruled for nearly four decades. His government faced the continuing financial and military demands of regional warfare.

### The Three-Polker Coin

The *półtorak*, literally meaning "one and a half," was a small silver coin valued at **1½ grosz**.

It was also commonly known as a **three-polker**, reflecting its equivalence to three half-grosz units.

These coins were produced in large quantities during the reign of Sigismund III, particularly at the **Bydgoszcz mint**, which became an important center of Polish coin production.

Their design was influenced by the three-kreuzer coinage of the Holy Roman Empire, with the prominent cross-topped orb serving as a recognizable feature.

The denomination was well suited to everyday commercial transactions and regional trade.

As a relatively small coin produced on a large scale, the three-polker illustrates the practical monetary needs of a major early modern European state.

### Heraldry and Political Identity

The coin's heraldic imagery reflects the Commonwealth's political structure.

The **White Eagle** represented the Kingdom of Poland, while the **Pursuer**, a mounted knight carrying a sword, represented the Grand Duchy of Lithuania.

The Vasa family arms at the center identified the ruling dynasty.

These symbols emphasized both the union of Poland and Lithuania and the authority of Sigismund III as their elected monarch.

The reverse inscription, identifying the issue as money of the Kingdom of Poland, reflects the administrative and monetary traditions that continued within the broader Commonwealth.

Together, the coin's designs illustrate the combination of dynastic monarchy, political federation, and regional commerce that characterized the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth during the early seventeenth century.
