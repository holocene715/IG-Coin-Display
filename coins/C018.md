This **square silver dirham** was issued by the **Almohad Caliphate**, a powerful medieval Islamic state that governed much of North Africa and the Iberian Peninsula during the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. Unlike the circular coins that dominated most Mediterranean and Islamic monetary traditions, the Almohads adopted an unusually distinctive square format for their silver dirhams.

The coin is **anonymous**, bearing no individual ruler's name or explicit date. Instead, its surfaces are filled with religious declarations written in Arabic. The seller attributes this specimen to **Fas (modern Fez, Morocco)** and dates it broadly to **1163–1269 CE**. The precise date cannot be determined from the standard inscriptions alone.

The obverse carries three lines emphasizing the **absolute unity and authority of God**, beginning with the Islamic declaration that there is no god but God. The reverse continues with three statements affirming God as Lord, Muhammad as Messenger, and **the Mahdi as Imam**. The reference to the Mahdi is especially significant because it expresses a central religious principle of the Almohad movement.

The inscriptions are arranged within square borders that echo the unusual shape of the coin itself. Unlike many earlier Islamic dirhams, which included circular marginal inscriptions identifying rulers, mints, and dates, the Almohad square dirham places its emphasis on a standardized religious message. Its bold, geometric design makes it immediately recognizable among the medieval coins of North Africa and Iberia.

Although this specimen is attributed to Fez, Almohad square dirhams were produced at multiple mints across the caliphate. Some varieties include small mint inscriptions, while others do not. The Fez attribution should therefore be considered provisional until the relevant inscription or diagnostic variety is verified.

## Historical Significance

The **Almohad Caliphate**, also known as the **Muwahhid dynasty**, originated among Amazigh (Berber) communities of the Atlas Mountains in present-day Morocco during the early twelfth century. Its founder, **Ibn Tumart**, preached a reformist interpretation of Islam emphasizing the absolute unity of God, or *tawhid*. His followers became known as the *al-Muwahhidun*, meaning "those who affirm the unity of God," from which the name Almohad is derived.

Ibn Tumart also claimed the title of **Mahdi**, a divinely guided religious leader. Although he died around 1130, his movement continued under his successor, **Abd al-Mu'min**, who transformed it into a major military and political power. By the middle of the twelfth century, the Almohads had conquered the territories of the preceding Almoravid dynasty, established control over Morocco, and extended their authority across much of North Africa and Muslim-ruled Iberia.

At its height, the Almohad Caliphate stretched from the Atlantic coast of Morocco across much of the Maghreb and into the Iberian Peninsula. Major cities including **Marrakesh, Fez, Seville, and Córdoba** became important centers of administration, commerce, scholarship, and religious life. The dynasty sponsored major architectural projects, including the **Koutoubia Mosque** in Marrakesh and the **Giralda** in Seville, whose surviving structures remain prominent examples of Almohad architecture.

The religious inscriptions on C018 offer a direct expression of the dynasty's founding ideology. Particularly important is the reverse declaration **"The Mahdi is our Imam,"** which refers to Ibn Tumart and the continuing authority of his religious teachings. Rather than identifying an individual reigning caliph, the coin emphasizes the theological principles that united the Almohad state.

The coin's **square format** also represents a significant departure from established Islamic monetary conventions. While most Islamic silver dirhams had been circular for centuries, Almohad silver coinage adopted a distinctive geometric form that visually separated it from earlier dynasties. This design was not merely decorative: it created an easily recognizable monetary type that circulated widely across the Almohad territories.

Almohad silver dirhams also influenced monetary practices beyond the caliphate's political boundaries. During the thirteenth century, Christian European merchants and authorities produced **imitations of Almohad square dirhams**, often called *millares*. These imitations circulated in commercial exchanges involving the western Mediterranean and North Africa, demonstrating the importance of Islamic silver currency in trade between Christian and Muslim societies.

The caliphate's political power began to decline following its defeat at the **Battle of Las Navas de Tolosa in 1212**, a major victory for the Christian kingdoms of Iberia. During the following decades, Almohad authority fragmented as regional dynasties emerged in North Africa and Christian kingdoms conquered much of the remaining Muslim territory in Iberia. The Almohad dynasty's final stronghold in Marrakesh fell to the **Marinids in 1269**.

C018 therefore represents an important intersection of **religious reform, political expansion, and monetary innovation**. Its compact square design embodies the ideological identity of the Almohad movement, while its circulation connects the commercial networks of medieval Morocco, al-Andalus, and the wider Mediterranean world.

Within this collection, C018 also provides a valuable geographic contrast with the preceding Qarakhanid and Central Asian Islamic coins. Although these dynasties were separated by thousands of miles, they shared the use of Arabic inscriptions and Islamic religious declarations on their currencies. The differences in their coin designs demonstrate how regional Islamic states developed distinctive monetary traditions while drawing upon a common religious and cultural framework.
