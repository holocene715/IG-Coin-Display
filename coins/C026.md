This square silver coin is attributed to an unidentified Christian issuing authority in **medieval western Europe**, probably in **Spain or southern France**, and dates to approximately **1250–1290 CE**. Its design imitates the distinctive square silver dirhams of the **Almohad Caliphate**, a Muslim dynasty that ruled extensive territories in North Africa and the Iberian Peninsula during the 12th and 13th centuries. The original Almohad dirhams (see C018) were notable for their **square shape and prominent Arabic religious inscriptions**, departing from the round format common to most contemporary European and Islamic coinage. 

The inscriptions on Almohad dirhams proclaimed fundamental Islamic beliefs, including the unity of God, the prophetic mission of Muhammad, and the religious authority of the Almohad movement's founder, Ibn Tumart, who was regarded by his followers as the Mahdi. C026 follows this general design but is attributed to a **Christian issuer rather than the Almohad state itself**. This distinction is important because the use of Arabic inscriptions on a medieval European coin does not necessarily establish that its issuer was Muslim. Arabic and Arabic-style lettering appeared on coins produced under several Christian authorities, sometimes preserving the original inscriptions and sometimes adapting them to different political or religious contexts. The coin's date range of approximately **1250–1290 CE** places it in the decades following the major Christian conquests of Almohad-held territories in Iberia, when Islamic and Christian monetary traditions continued to overlap.

## Historical Significance

The **Almohad Caliphate** originated as a religious reform movement among Berber communities of the Atlas Mountains of Morocco in the 12th century. The Almohads established a powerful state extending across much of North Africa and the southern Iberian Peninsula. During the late 12th century, the Almohads were among the most formidable political and military powers in the western Mediterranean. Their territories included major commercial and cultural centers such as Marrakesh, Fez, Seville, and Córdoba. However, their authority in Iberia weakened dramatically following the **Battle of Las Navas de Tolosa in 1212 CE**, when a coalition of Christian kingdoms defeated a major Almohad army. Over the following decades, Christian rulers conquered much of the remaining Muslim-controlled territory in the Iberian Peninsula. **Córdoba fell to Castile in 1236**, followed by **Valencia in 1238** and **Seville in 1248**. By the middle of the 13th century, the Nasrid Emirate of Granada remained the principal independent Muslim state in Iberia.

These political transformations did not immediately eliminate the economic and cultural connections that had developed between Christian and Muslim communities. The imitation of foreign coinage was a widespread practice in medieval Europe and the Mediterranean. Coins that had become familiar and trusted in commercial exchange were sometimes reproduced by neighboring authorities, particularly when their weight, metal content, or recognizable design facilitated trade. Islamic coins were especially influential in regions connected to Mediterranean commerce. Their designs were **imitated by Christian rulers in Iberia, southern Italy, and other parts of Europe**, where merchants and consumers were accustomed to handling currency from multiple political and religious traditions.

The square silver dirhams of the Almohads provide a distinctive example of this phenomenon. Their unusual shape and calligraphic designs could remain recognizable even when reproduced outside the political authority of the original issuing state. Such imitations may have served several purposes. They could facilitate trade with populations familiar with Islamic currency, supplement local monetary supplies, or take advantage of an established coin type's commercial reputation.

In some cases, Christian authorities reproduced Islamic formulas with remarkable fidelity, even when the text expressed beliefs that differed from those of the issuing ruler. In other cases, the inscriptions became increasingly stylized, abbreviated, or distorted. Engravers unfamiliar with Arabic could reproduce the general appearance of the lettering without preserving a fully legible text. In the case of C026, the writing is crude and does not read clearly as Arabic.
