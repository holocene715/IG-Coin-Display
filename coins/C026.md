This square silver coin is attributed to an **unidentified Christian issuing authority in medieval western Europe**, probably in **Spain or southern France**, and dates to approximately **1250–1290 CE**. Its design imitates the distinctive square silver dirhams of the **Almohad Caliphate**, a Muslim dynasty that ruled extensive territories in North Africa and the Iberian Peninsula during the twelfth and thirteenth centuries.

The original Almohad dirhams were notable for their **square shape and prominent Arabic religious inscriptions**, departing from the round format common to most contemporary European and Islamic coinage. Their distinctive appearance made them readily recognizable in commercial exchange.

The inscriptions on Almohad dirhams proclaimed fundamental Islamic beliefs, including the unity of God, the prophetic mission of Muhammad, and the religious authority of the Almohad movement's founder, Ibn Tumart, who was regarded by his followers as the Mahdi.

The present coin follows this general design but is attributed to a **Christian issuer rather than the Almohad state itself**. Its exact issuing authority, mint, and inscriptional details remain uncertain. It may have reproduced the Arabic formulas of the original dirhams or employed altered lettering intended primarily to resemble the familiar Almohad type.

This distinction is important because the use of Arabic inscriptions on a medieval European coin does not necessarily establish that its issuer was Muslim. Arabic and Arabic-style lettering appeared on coins produced under several Christian authorities, sometimes preserving the original inscriptions and sometimes adapting them to different political or religious contexts.

The coin's date range of approximately **1250–1290 CE** places it in the decades following the major Christian conquests of Almohad-held territories in Iberia, when Islamic and Christian monetary traditions continued to overlap.

## Historical Significance

### The Almohads and Medieval Iberia

The **Almohad Caliphate** originated as a religious reform movement among Berber communities of the Atlas Mountains in the twelfth century. Under the leadership of Abd al-Mu'min and his successors, the Almohads established a powerful state extending across much of North Africa and the southern Iberian Peninsula.

During the late twelfth century, the Almohads were among the most formidable political and military powers in the western Mediterranean. Their territories included major commercial and cultural centers such as Marrakesh, Seville, Córdoba, and Fez.

However, their authority in Iberia weakened dramatically following the **Battle of Las Navas de Tolosa in 1212 CE**, when a coalition of Christian kingdoms defeated a major Almohad army.

Over the following decades, Christian rulers conquered much of the remaining Muslim-controlled territory in the Iberian Peninsula. **Córdoba fell to Castile in 1236**, followed by **Valencia in 1238** and **Seville in 1248**. By the middle of the thirteenth century, the Nasrid Emirate of Granada remained the principal independent Muslim state in Iberia.

These political transformations did not immediately eliminate the economic and cultural connections that had developed between Christian and Muslim communities.

### Imitation Coinage Across Religious Boundaries

The imitation of foreign coinage was a widespread practice in medieval Europe and the Mediterranean. Coins that had become familiar and trusted in commercial exchange were sometimes reproduced by neighboring authorities, particularly when their weight, metal content, or recognizable design facilitated trade.

Islamic coins were especially influential in regions connected to Mediterranean commerce. Their designs were imitated by Christian rulers in Iberia, southern Italy, and other parts of Europe, where merchants and consumers were accustomed to handling currency from multiple political and religious traditions.

The square silver dirhams of the Almohads provide a distinctive example of this phenomenon. Their unusual shape and calligraphic designs could remain recognizable even when reproduced outside the political authority of the original issuing state.

Such imitations may have served several purposes. They could facilitate trade with populations familiar with Islamic currency, supplement local monetary supplies, or take advantage of an established coin type's commercial reputation.

The motivations behind any particular imitation, however, depend on its issuing authority, date, metal content, and archaeological context. Without a secure mint attribution, the precise circumstances surrounding C026's production remain uncertain.

### Arabic Inscriptions on Christian Coinage

One of the most intriguing aspects of Christian imitations of Islamic coins is the **continued use of Arabic religious inscriptions**.

In some cases, Christian authorities reproduced Islamic formulas with remarkable fidelity, even when the text expressed beliefs that differed from those of the issuing ruler. This demonstrates that coin designs could acquire commercial significance beyond their original religious and political meanings.

In other cases, the inscriptions became increasingly stylized, abbreviated, or distorted. Engravers unfamiliar with Arabic could reproduce the general appearance of the lettering without preserving a fully legible text.

Arabic inscriptions also appeared on some Christian coins for reasons unrelated to simple imitation. In medieval Iberia, Arabic remained an important language of administration, scholarship, and everyday communication among diverse communities.

Consequently, the presence of Arabic writing must be interpreted in relation to the coin's broader historical and numismatic context rather than treated as definitive evidence of the issuer's religion.

### A Comparison with C018

C026 is particularly interesting when compared with **C018**, an original Almohad square silver dirham attributed to Fez, Morocco.

C018 represents the monetary traditions of the Almohad Caliphate itself, with inscriptions expressing the religious ideology of the Almohad movement. C026, by contrast, represents the **adaptation of that monetary design beyond the political boundaries of the Almohad state**.

Together, the two coins illustrate how monetary forms could survive changes in political control and circulate across cultural and religious boundaries.

Their comparison also provides an opportunity to examine differences in calligraphy, flan preparation, engraving style, and the arrangement of inscriptions. Such details may help distinguish official Almohad issues from contemporary or later imitations.

Ultimately, C026 represents the interconnected commercial world of the **medieval western Mediterranean**, where political and religious divisions did not prevent the exchange of goods, artistic traditions, and monetary practices.

Its square shape and Arabic-inspired inscriptions preserve the visual legacy of Islamic coinage within a Christian European context, offering a tangible example of the complex cultural relationships that shaped medieval Spain and southern France.
