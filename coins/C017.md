This large, broad **dirham** was issued by **Jalal al-Din Muhammad ibn Nasr**, a regional Qarakhanid ruler based at **Kasan**, in the northern Ferghana Valley of Central Asia. The coin is attributed to **AH 598 (1201–1202 CE)**, near the end of his rule. Its design belongs to a distinctive late Qarakhanid monetary tradition characterized by large, thin coins with extensive Arabic inscriptions and relatively little or no figurative decoration.

Unlike the earlier Islamic silver dirhams, which were generally struck in high-purity silver and followed relatively standardized weights, many late Qarakhanid dirhams were produced in copper or copper-rich alloys containing variable amounts of silver. The obverse and reverse are dominated by **Arabic inscriptions**, continuing a visual tradition established centuries earlier in Islamic coinage. The inscriptions communicated religious declarations, the issuing ruler's authority, and information about the coin's production. The relatively large surface of these broad dirhams provided space for elaborate arrangements of text, making the inscriptions themselves the principal decorative elements.

The coin was struck at **Kasan**, generally identified with modern **Kosonsoy** in Uzbekistan, near the border with Kyrgyzstan. Situated along the northern Ferghana Valley, Kasan was one of several regional centers that issued coinage under the later Qarakhanids. Its location placed it within a network of agricultural settlements, mountain passes, and trade routes linking the Ferghana Valley with the wider cities and markets of Central Asia.

## Historical Significance

The **Qarakhanids** were a Turkic ruling dynasty that emerged in Central Asia during the tenth century and played a major role in the region's transition from Samanid to Turkic political rule. After adopting Islam, Qarakhanid rulers gradually extended their authority over the cities and agricultural regions of Transoxiana and the Ferghana Valley. Their conquest of Bukhara in **999 CE** marked the end of Samanid rule there and a major turning point in the political history of Islamic Central Asia. Over the following two centuries, Qarakhanid political authority became increasingly divided among branches of the ruling family and smaller regional principalities. These rulers often acknowledged the authority of more powerful neighboring states while retaining control over local cities, agricultural districts, and mints. By the late 12th century, the Qarakhanid political landscape was substantially different from the expanding kingdom represented by the earlier **C016 fals of Mansur ibn Ahmad**, struck at Ilaq in 998 CE.

**Jalal al-Din Muhammad ibn Nasr**, also known as Tughril Khan, was one of these later regional rulers. He succeeded his father, Nasr ibn al-Husayn, and exercised authority at **Kasan**, with his documented activity extending from approximately **1178 to 1202 CE**. During this period, Central Asia was increasingly influenced by the **Qara Khitai (Western Liao)**, whose rulers established political supremacy over much of the region during the 12th century. The Qarakhanids continued to govern many local territories under this larger political framework. At the same time, the **Khwarazmshahs** were expanding eastward from Khwarazm, eventually challenging both Qara Khitai and Qarakhanid authority. Within little more than a decade of C017's issue, the political order that had supported the Qarakhanids for centuries would undergo profound changes.

For this collection, **C017 provides a particularly valuable comparison with C016**. Both coins were issued by Qarakhanid authorities in the wider region of modern Uzbekistan, yet they are separated by more than two centuries. C016 represents the period of Qarakhanid expansion and the displacement of Samanid authority, while C017 belongs to the dynasty's later, fragmented political landscape. Together, they illustrate the remarkable longevity of Qarakhanid rule and the transformation of Central Asian coinage between the tenth and early thirteenth centuries. C017 also stands near the end of an important historical era. Within two decades of its issue, the **Mongol conquests of Central Asia** would radically transform the political and economic networks that had shaped the region for centuries. The coin therefore represents one of the final phases of the pre-Mongol Islamic monetary traditions of the Ferghana Valley.
