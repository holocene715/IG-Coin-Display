This silver drachm was issued under **Phraates IV**, ruler of the Parthian Empire during the late first century BCE. The obverse depicts the king in the distinctive Parthian royal portrait style, wearing a diadem and segmented necklet. This specimen is particularly notable for the **countermark impressed over the lower portion of the royal portrait**, evidence that the coin continued to circulate and was subsequently marked after its original striking. The reverse depicts the traditional **seated Parthian archer**, an image ultimately associated with Arsakes I, founder of the Arsacid dynasty. Despite the distinctly Iranian political identity and imagery of the coin, its extensive royal titulature is written entirely in **Greek**. Particularly notable is the title **ΦΙΛΕΛΛΗΝΟΣ (Philhellenos)**, meaning “Friend of the Greeks.”

The small oval bust on the obverse is a countermark, or a stamp made after the coin had already been in circulation. This was often done when a new entity claimed authority over new territory but wanted to keep existing currency in circulation. In this case, the countermark resembles the style of Bactrian coinage (see C002) and may indicate Bactrian influence over the local governors at this time. 

## Historical Significance

The Parthian Empire arose in the third century BCE in what is now northeastern Iran and grew into a powerful rival of Rome. For nearly five centuries, it ruled a vast region between the Mediterranean world and Central Asia, drawing wealth from trade and combining Iranian traditions with Greek influences. In 224 CE, the Sasanian dynasty replaced the Parthian rulers (see coin C011). 

**Phraates IV** ruled the Parthian Empire at a time when it had become one of the principal powers of the ancient Near East and the major eastern rival of Rome. His reign followed decades of Roman-Parthian conflict, including the catastrophic Roman defeat at Carrhae in 53 BCE. In 20 BCE, Phraates reached a diplomatic settlement with **Augustus** and returned the military standards captured from Roman armies during earlier conflicts. Rome celebrated their recovery extensively in its own political imagery, making the episode an important moment in the developing relationship between the Roman and Parthian worlds.

This coin also illustrates the extraordinary longevity of **Greek cultural influence in Central and Western Asia**. More than three centuries after Alexander the Great's conquests, an Iranian dynasty ruling a vast empire from Mesopotamia to Central Asia continued to place Greek inscriptions and Hellenistic royal terminology, albeit crude and jumbled, on its silver currency. The title *Philhellenos*, “Friend of the Greeks,” is particularly evocative of this legacy. Greek had ceased to represent Greek political control in the region; instead, it had become one element of the multilingual and multicultural political vocabulary inherited and adapted by the Parthian state.
