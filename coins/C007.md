This billon tetradrachm was issued under **Gondophares I**, founder and one of the best-known rulers of the Indo-Parthian Kingdom. His realm developed across territories encompassing parts of present-day Afghanistan, Pakistan, and northwestern India during the first century CE.

The coin provides a particularly clear example of the cultural mixture characteristic of the Indo-Parthian world. The obverse presents Gondophares in a portrait tradition descended from Hellenistic coinage and surrounds him with a **Greek inscription**. The reverse depicts **Nike**, the Greek personification of victory, but accompanies the image with a **Prakrit inscription written in Kharosthi script**. Greek and South Asian linguistic traditions therefore appear together on a coin issued by a dynasty of Iranian origin.

The type is attributed to **Arachosia**, an ancient region centered on what is now southern Afghanistan. The precise mint is uncertain, so the map marker represents Arachosia generally rather than a securely identified mint city.

## Historical Significance

**Gondophares I** was the founder and most prominent ruler associated with the Indo-Parthian dynasty, which established control across a broad zone extending from **Seistan and Arachosia through the Kabul region and into the northwestern Indian subcontinent**. This placed the Indo-Parthians at one of antiquity's major cultural crossroads, where Iranian, Hellenistic, Central Asian, and South Asian traditions interacted. Because surviving written evidence for the Indo-Parthians is limited, their coins are among the most important sources for reconstructing their political history.

This coin is especially appropriate to the **Lasting Greek Influence** section of the collection. By the first century CE, direct Greek political rule in Arachosia had disappeared, yet Greek remained part of the language and visual vocabulary of royal coinage. Gondophares paired a Greek royal title and the unmistakably Greek figure of **Nike** with Kharosthi writing and an Indo-Parthian political identity. Rather than representing continued Greek rule, the coin demonstrates how Hellenistic traditions were absorbed and repurposed by the successor cultures of Central and South Asia.
