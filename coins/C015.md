This copper **fals** was issued during the reign of **Nuh II ibn Mansur (976–997 CE)**, a ruler of the **Samanid Empire**. Its attribution to a mint in the **Ferghana Valley** is provisional, based on the information supplied with the coin and comparison with known Samanid issues. The precise mint and year have not yet been established from the surviving marginal inscriptions.

Both sides are dominated by **Arabic calligraphy** rather than portraits or figurative designs. Lines of inscription occupy the central fields, enclosed by beaded circular borders, while additional writing runs around the margins. The coin's uneven edge, surface wear, and partial loss of lettering are characteristic challenges when identifying individual hammered copper issues. The surviving design is consistent with the epigraphic style of Samanid coinage, in which statements of Islamic faith and the names of political authorities were central elements of the monetary design.

The obverse is provisionally associated with the profession of divine unity:

**لا إله إلا الله وحده لا شريك له**

*There is no god but God alone; He has no partner.*

The reverse is provisionally associated with the declaration **محمد رسول الله** (*Muhammad is the Messenger of God*) and the name **نوح بن منصور** (*Nuh ibn Mansur*). These readings describe the attributed coinage; **they should not be treated as a complete transcription of this particular specimen**. A secure identification of the mint and date will require closer comparison of the marginal legends with published examples.

## Historical Significance

The **Samanid Empire** was a major Iranian-led Muslim state that governed much of **Transoxiana and Khurasan** during the ninth and tenth centuries. Its rulers maintained connections with the Abbasid caliphs while exercising substantial independent political authority. Under Samanid patronage, cities such as **Bukhara and Samarkand** became important centers of scholarship, trade, and the development of **New Persian** literature and court culture. The dynasty's coinage, however, was predominantly inscribed in **Arabic**, reflecting the language's central role in Islamic religious and monetary expression.

**Nuh II ibn Mansur** ruled during a period of growing pressure on the Samanid state. Regional military commanders and rival dynasties challenged the authority of the court, while the **Qarakhanids** expanded their influence in Central Asia. The Samanid political order eventually collapsed shortly after Nuh II's death, when the Qarakhanids took Bukhara in **999 CE**. A fals from his reign therefore belongs to the final decades of a dynasty that had helped shape the cultural and political landscape of Islamic Central Asia.

The **Ferghana Valley**, enclosed by mountain ranges and linked to the wider Central Asian trade network, was a valuable agricultural and commercial region. Its towns connected routes between Transoxiana, the eastern steppe, and lands farther toward China. If the proposed Ferghana attribution is confirmed, C015 would offer a tangible example of the **regional copper currency** used within this eastern part of the Samanid realm. Unlike the silver dirhams that traveled over great distances and reached Viking Age hoards in northern Europe, copper fals were generally more important for **local and regional transactions**.

For this collection, C015 illustrates how thoroughly **Arabic inscription-based coinage** had become established in Central Asia by the late tenth century. Its appearance contrasts sharply with the Sasanian-derived portraits, fire altars, and Pahlavi writing on earlier Iranian coins, yet the issuing dynasty itself was deeply rooted in Iranian cultural traditions. The coin thus reflects an important theme of the display: **political, religious, linguistic, and artistic change did not always proceed together**, and a Persianate state could express its monetary authority almost entirely through Arabic script.
