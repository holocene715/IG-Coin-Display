## Historical Background

Frederick III (1609–1670) ruled Denmark–Norway from 1648 until his death in 1670, succeeding his father, **Christian IV**, whose long reign had been marked by ambitious construction projects, maritime commerce, and costly military campaigns. By the late 1660s, Frederick had substantially transformed the monarchy he inherited. Whereas Christian IV had governed within Denmark's traditional elective system, Frederick established hereditary royal succession and centralized authority, fundamentally altering the relationship between the crown and the nobility.

The greatest crisis of Frederick's reign came during the **Dano-Swedish Wars of 1657–1660**. Sweden, under Charles X Gustav, invaded Denmark and forced the humiliating Treaty of Roskilde in 1658, through which Denmark surrendered Scania, Halland, Blekinge, and other territories. When Swedish forces renewed their invasion, Frederick remained in Copenhagen and helped organize its defense during a prolonged siege. With support from the Dutch Republic, Denmark resisted conquest, and the **Treaty of Copenhagen in 1660** ended the conflict, restoring some territories while confirming Sweden's possession of the southern Scandinavian provinces.

The political upheaval following these wars enabled Frederick to introduce **hereditary absolutism in 1660**, ending the centuries-old practice of electing Danish monarchs. The change was consolidated by the **King's Law of 1665**, which established extensive royal authority and became one of the defining constitutional documents of early modern Europe. Frederick also expanded centralized administration and weakened the political privileges of the hereditary nobility. These reforms aligned Denmark–Norway with a wider European trend toward stronger centralized monarchies, particularly under rulers such as **Louis XIV of France**.

Norway remained an integral but politically subordinate kingdom within the Danish–Norwegian monarchy, governed largely through institutions centered in Copenhagen. Nevertheless, Norway retained its own heraldic identity and important regional economic interests, including timber exports, shipping, mining, and fisheries. The **Christiania mint**, located in the city founded by Christian IV in 1624, produced coinage bearing the Norwegian lion holding Saint Olaf's axe. This emblem distinguished Norwegian issues from coins produced in Copenhagen, which more commonly displayed the three lions of Denmark.

This **1668 three-skilling coin** was minted near the end of Frederick III's reign, eight years after the establishment of hereditary monarchy. Its Norwegian lion and Christiania mint connect it to the northern kingdom, while its royal inscriptions reflect the continuing political union with Denmark. Displayed alongside the **1648 Copenhagen two-skilling (C034)**, it illustrates two decades of profound change: the decline of Denmark's Baltic dominance, the consolidation of Sweden's regional power, and Frederick III's transformation from an elected monarch into an absolute hereditary ruler.
