## About This Coin

This brass accounting jeton was manufactured in Nuremberg during the late 16th century by Chilianus Kochuus, whose initials appear beneath the figures on its reverse. It imitates a French jeton associated with André Hac, a clerk of the French Court of Monies. Its imagery combines Hac's personal coat of arms with an allegorical representation of French strength and unity, illustrating the international market for accounting tokens in Renaissance Europe.

## Historical Significance

This jeton represents the intersection of German craftsmanship and French monetary administration during the Renaissance. Although its design identifies a French royal official, it was manufactured in the Imperial Free City of Nuremberg, where specialized workshops produced accounting tokens for customers throughout Europe. The imitation of an official French design demonstrates how heraldic and political imagery could circulate independently of the institutions that originally commissioned it.

## Historical Background

During the 16th century, Nuremberg was one of the Holy Roman Empire's most prosperous commercial and manufacturing centers. As an Imperial Free City, it enjoyed considerable autonomy while remaining under the authority of the emperor. Its metalworkers became particularly renowned for producing brass accounting jetons, known in German as *Rechenpfennige*. These small tokens were widely exported and used by merchants, bankers, and government officials for calculations on counting boards. Unlike coins, jetons generally had no assigned monetary value and were not intended to circulate as legal tender.

The French *Cour des Monnaies*, or Court of Monies, was a royal institution responsible for regulating coinage and adjudicating monetary disputes. Its jurisdiction included oversight of mints, enforcement of monetary ordinances, and prosecution of offenses involving coinage. André Hac served as a clerk of this institution, and jetons bearing his name and coat of arms reflect the prominence of administrative officials within France's increasingly complex financial bureaucracy. The three birds depicted on the shield are heraldic lapwings, identifying the arms associated with Hac.

The reverse presents two standing figures clasping hands across a flowering lily branch, accompanied by a rooster and a dog. The surrounding Latin inscription, *GALLIA FORTIT*, invokes the strength or valor of France. The lily and rooster reinforce the French association, while the joined hands suggest concord, loyalty, or political unity. Beneath the scene appear the initials C.K., identifying the Nuremberg jeton maker Chilianus Kochuus. This combination of French allegorical imagery and a German maker's mark reflects the cross-border production of Renaissance tokens.

Nuremberg manufacturers frequently copied or adapted designs originally produced for foreign officials, institutions, and rulers. Their workshops developed a substantial international trade in inexpensive brass jetons, supplying markets from France and the Low Countries to England and beyond. The André Hac type is particularly noteworthy because it reproduces the imagery of an official associated with monetary regulation. Although the token was not itself circulating currency, its imitation of an administrative jeton demonstrates the commercial value of recognizable official designs.

By the 17th century, written arithmetic and evolving bookkeeping practices gradually reduced the practical importance of counting-board jetons. Nevertheless, jeton production continued, increasingly serving commemorative, decorative, and promotional purposes. Surviving examples provide a record of early modern European commerce, heraldry, political symbolism, and manufacturing networks. C036 illustrates how an object made in a German imperial city could preserve the name and imagery of a French royal official, connecting two distinct political and economic worlds.
