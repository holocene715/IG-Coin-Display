Manufactured in Nuremberg during the late 16th century, this brass accounting jeton imitates a French issue associated with André Hac, a clerk of the royal Court of Monies. Its obverse bears Hac's coat of arms, featuring three lapwings, while its reverse depicts two figures clasping hands beneath the inscription *GALLIA FORTIT*, invoking the strength of France. Produced by the Nuremberg jeton maker Chilianus Kochuus, whose initials appear beneath the figures, the piece illustrates the international exchange of commercial objects, heraldic imagery, and political symbolism during the Renaissance.

## Historical Background

During the 16th century, **Nuremberg** was one of the most prosperous commercial and manufacturing centers of the **Holy Roman Empire**. As an Imperial Free City, it enjoyed substantial political autonomy under the authority of the emperor, allowing its merchants and craftsmen to participate in extensive European trading networks. Among its most successful industries was the manufacture of brass accounting jetons, known in German as *Rechenpfennige*. These small tokens were produced in enormous quantities and exported throughout Europe, where merchants, bankers, and government administrators used them to perform calculations on counting boards. Unlike circulating coins, jetons generally had no official monetary value and were not intended to function as legal tender.

The **French Cour des Monnaies**, or Court of Monies, was a royal institution responsible for overseeing coinage, supervising mints, and adjudicating monetary disputes and offenses. As royal government expanded during the Renaissance, specialized financial institutions such as the Court of Monies became increasingly important to the administration of the French kingdom. **André Hac**, whose name appears on the obverse of this jeton, served as a clerk of the court. His heraldic arms, featuring three lapwings arranged two above and one below, occupy the central design. Such personal and institutional imagery was common on French jetons, which could serve practical accounting functions while also identifying officials and their administrative authority.

The reverse presents an allegorical scene of **two standing warriors clasping hands across a flowering lily branch**, accompanied by a rooster and a dog. The surrounding Latin inscription, *GALLIA FORTIT*, invokes the strength or valor of France. The lily was closely associated with the French monarchy, while the rooster had longstanding associations with Gaul and France. The joined hands suggest concord, cooperation, or political unity, themes of particular importance during the **French Wars of Religion (1562–1598)**, when conflict between Catholics and Protestants repeatedly destabilized the kingdom. Beneath the figures appear the initials **C.K.**, identifying the Nuremberg jeton maker **Chilianus Kochuus**.

Nuremberg's jeton manufacturers developed a substantial international trade by adapting and imitating designs originally produced for foreign rulers, officials, and institutions. This jeton is especially noteworthy because it reproduces the imagery of an official associated with the regulation of coinage and monetary administration. Its German manufacture and French administrative subject demonstrate that the political identity represented on a jeton did not necessarily correspond to its place of production. Rather, recognizable heraldic designs and inscriptions could be reproduced for a broad commercial market, reflecting the interconnected economies of Renaissance Europe.

By the 17th century, the growing use of written arithmetic and increasingly sophisticated bookkeeping practices gradually reduced the practical importance of counting-board jetons. Nevertheless, Nuremberg continued producing tokens for accounting, commemorative, and decorative purposes. Surviving examples preserve evidence of early modern commercial practices, political allegory, heraldry, and international manufacturing networks. This **André Hac jeton** illustrates how a modest brass token could connect the Imperial Free City of Nuremberg with the institutions of the French monarchy, embodying the commercial and cultural exchanges that characterized Renaissance Europe.
