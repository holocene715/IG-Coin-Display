This silver **mezzo giulio**, or **half giulio**, was struck in **Rome** during the pontificate of **Pope Clement VII (1523–1534 CE)**. Clement, born **Giulio de' Medici**, belonged to the powerful Florentine family whose influence extended from banking and civic politics to the highest offices of the Catholic Church. The coin is undated, so its precise year of manufacture within his pontificate cannot be established from the design alone.

The **obverse** bears the distinctive **Medici coat of arms**, recognizable by its six roundels, beneath the **crossed keys of Saint Peter** and the **papal tiara**. The keys symbolize the spiritual authority traditionally associated with Saint Peter and his successors, while the tiara represents the papal office. Their placement above the Medici shield unites Clement's personal dynastic identity with his position as head of the Church and ruler of the Papal States.

The **reverse** features a large cross separating the letters **R O M A** into four quarters. Its surrounding inscription, **FIAT PAX IN VIRTVTE TVA**, invokes peace within the walls of the city, drawing on Psalm 122:7. In a period of intense political and military conflict, the appeal to peace was a particularly resonant expression of Rome's religious and civic identity.

The linked CGB reference illustrates this type and records a specimen weighing approximately **1.63 g** and measuring **22.8 mm**; those are reference measurements, not measurements of this coin.

## Historical Significance

### A Medici Pope

**Clement VII** was elected pope in **1523**, following the brief pontificate of Adrian VI. A member of the Medici family, he had risen through the Church hierarchy during the pontificate of his cousin **Leo X**, another Medici pope. His election reflected the extraordinary reach of Florentine political and financial influence in Renaissance Italy.

Although the papacy was a religious institution, the pope also ruled the **Papal States**, a substantial territory in central Italy. The papal court therefore operated within the competitive politics of the Italian peninsula, balancing relations with France, the Holy Roman Empire, Venice, Florence, and other powers.

The coin's Medici shield makes this dual identity unusually explicit. It identifies the issuing pope not through a portrait but through **heraldry**, linking a specific family to the universal claims of the papal office.

### The Sack of Rome in 1527

Clement VII's pontificate is inseparable from the **Sack of Rome in May 1527**, one of the defining catastrophes of Renaissance Italy. Imperial forces associated with **Charles V**, many of them unpaid and operating with limited discipline, entered Rome and subjected the city to extensive violence, plunder, and destruction.

Clement took refuge in **Castel Sant'Angelo**, where he endured a siege before reaching terms with the imperial forces. The sack severely damaged Rome's population, economy, and cultural life, while exposing the vulnerability of the papacy as an Italian territorial power.

The reverse prayer for peace is especially striking in light of these events. However, because this coin type is **undated**, it would be misleading to claim that the inscription was specifically created in response to the sack. Its language expresses a broader biblical ideal that acquired particular poignancy during Clement's troubled reign.

### Papal Authority and the Protestant Reformation

Clement's reign unfolded during the early **Protestant Reformation**, as challenges to papal authority spread across parts of northern Europe. He also faced the increasingly consequential dispute over **Henry VIII of England's request to annul his marriage to Catherine of Aragon**. Clement's refusal to grant the annulment contributed to the political and religious rupture that culminated in England's break with papal jurisdiction.

These events illustrate the difficult position of a Renaissance pope who was simultaneously a spiritual leader, an Italian ruler, and a participant in European dynastic diplomacy.

### The Giulio and Papal Silver Coinage

The **giulio** was a silver denomination introduced under **Pope Julius II** in the early sixteenth century. Its name commemorated that pope, and fractional denominations such as the **mezzo giulio** provided useful currency for transactions below the value of a full giulio.

Papal coins served practical monetary needs while communicating authority through religious imagery, inscriptions, and heraldic devices. This mezzo giulio is a compact example of that combination: the **Medici arms and papal regalia** proclaim who issued the coin, while the **cross and prayer for Rome** express the religious and civic ideals associated with the papacy.

### Rome Between Medieval Tradition and Renaissance Politics

C028 occupies a revealing position in the collection's chronology. Its imagery belongs to a long tradition of Christian coinage, yet its political context is distinctly **Renaissance**: the rise of powerful banking families, competition among territorial states, and the increasingly international consequences of religious conflict.

The coin also offers a contrast with the Venetian issues in the collection. While the **Venetian grosso of Giovanni Soranzo (C027)** presents an elected doge receiving authority from Saint Mark, Clement VII's mezzo giulio displays the heraldic identity of a pope who combined religious leadership with temporal sovereignty.

Together, these coins illustrate two very different forms of political legitimacy in Italy: the institutional identity of a maritime republic and the spiritual and territorial authority of the papacy.
