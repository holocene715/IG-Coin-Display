This bronze coin of **Licinius II**, struck at **Cyzicus between 317 and 320 CE**, depicts the young Roman Caesar on the obverse and **Jupiter**, chief deity of the Roman pantheon, on the reverse. Jupiter stands holding a scepter and a small figure of Victory supported on a globe, accompanied by the inscription **IOVI CONSERVATORI CAESS**, meaning "To Jupiter, Protector of the Caesars." The coin reflects the political and religious symbolism of the early fourth-century Roman Empire, when the imperial government was divided among several rulers and competing claims to authority increasingly shaped Roman politics.

## Historical Background

**Licinius II**, also known as **Licinius Junior**, was the son of Emperor Licinius I and Constantia, the half-sister of Emperor Constantine I. His father had risen to power during the final years of the Tetrarchy, the system of shared imperial government established by Diocletian in 293 CE (see C040). Following the collapse of the original tetrarchic arrangements, Licinius I and Constantine emerged as the two principal rulers of the Roman Empire. Their relationship was initially strengthened by the marriage of Licinius to Constantia in 313 CE, an alliance that helped establish a temporary political settlement between the eastern and western imperial courts. Nevertheless, the two emperors remained rivals, and their competing ambitions soon produced military conflict. In 316–317 CE, Constantine and Licinius fought a series of engagements that resulted in a negotiated settlement and a reorganization of imperial authority. As part of that agreement, **Licinius II was elevated to the rank of Caesar on March 1, 317 CE**, alongside Constantine's sons Crispus and Constantine II. Although still a young child, Licinius II became an official member of the imperial government, representing his father's dynastic ambitions and the continuing importance of hereditary succession within the Roman political system.

The political settlement between Constantine and Licinius ultimately proved temporary. Their rivalry intensified during the early 320s, culminating in a renewed civil war in **324 CE**. Constantine defeated Licinius at the battles of Adrianople and Chrysopolis, bringing the entire Roman Empire under his sole rule. Licinius I surrendered and was initially spared, but was executed in 325 CE after being accused of plotting against Constantine. **Licinius II**, despite his youth and family relationship to Constantine, was stripped of his rank following his father's defeat and subsequently executed, probably in 326 CE. His death marked the elimination of a potential dynastic rival and demonstrated the precarious position of imperial heirs during periods of political conflict. Constantine went on to establish a new imperial capital at **Constantinople in 330 CE**, fundamentally reshaping the political geography of the Roman world. This coin of Licinius II preserves a moment when the Roman Empire was governed through a fragile arrangement of competing imperial families, and when traditional symbols of divine protection were used to reinforce the legitimacy of rulers whose political futures remained deeply uncertain.

The coin was produced at **Cyzicus**, an important Greek city situated on the southern coast of the Sea of Marmara in northwestern Anatolia. Founded during the early period of Greek colonization, Cyzicus developed into a prosperous commercial center because of its location along maritime routes connecting the Aegean Sea, the Black Sea, and the interior of Asia Minor. The city was particularly famous in antiquity for its electrum coinage, including the widely circulated **Cyzicene staters** of the classical Greek world. Under Roman rule, Cyzicus remained an important regional center and eventually became the site of an imperial mint. During the late third and early fourth centuries, the mint produced substantial quantities of bronze coinage for the Roman government, serving the military, administrative, and commercial needs of the eastern provinces. The mint's location near the Bosporus and the approaches to the Black Sea gave it strategic importance during the period when imperial power was increasingly concentrated in the eastern Mediterranean.
