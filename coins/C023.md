This large bronze **dirham** was issued during the reign of **Najm al-Din Alpi**, a ruler of the **Artuqid dynasty of Mardin** in southeastern Anatolia. Alpi ruled from **1152 to 1176 CE (AH 547–572)**, during a period when the region was divided among competing Turkic, Kurdish, Arab, Armenian, and Byzantine powers.

The Artuqids were a Turkic dynasty that emerged in the aftermath of the Seljuk expansion into Anatolia and northern Mesopotamia. Their territories included important cities and trade routes linking the Anatolian plateau with Syria, Iraq, and the upper Tigris and Euphrates river valleys.

The **obverse** depicts two male busts facing one another. Both wear diadems and draped garments, recalling the portraiture of earlier Roman and Byzantine coins. Arabic inscriptions above and below the figures identify the honorific titles of Najm al-Din Alpi. A small **tamgha**, or dynastic emblem, appears beside the portraits, reflecting the continued importance of Turkic symbols of political identity.

The **reverse** presents an especially unusual scene: a standing Byzantine emperor being crowned by the **Virgin Mary**, known in Byzantine Christianity as the *Theotokos*, or "Mother of God." This composition draws directly upon Byzantine imperial imagery, in which divine or saintly figures were often shown bestowing legitimacy upon a ruler.

Despite the Christian imagery, the coin was issued under a **Muslim Turkic ruler**. An Arabic inscription surrounding the figures records the genealogy of Najm al-Din Alpi, asserting the authority of the Artuqid dynasty. The result is a striking combination of Byzantine visual traditions and Islamic political inscriptions.

The precise mint and year of this issue are uncertain. The type is generally associated with the Artuqid branch ruling from **Mardin**, in present-day southeastern Turkey. The corresponding CNG catalog example is undated and lists the mint as uncertain, possibly Mardin.

The reference specimen is classified as **Whelan Type II, 40–41; Spengler and Sayles Type 28; and Album 1827.3**. Its recorded dimensions of 35.5 mm and 15.45 g are not measurements of this particular coin.

## Historical Significance

The **Artuqids** were a dynasty of Oghuz Turkic origin that established several principalities in southeastern Anatolia and northern Mesopotamia following the fragmentation of Seljuk political authority. Their name derives from **Artuq ibn Eksük**, a military commander who served the Seljuks during the late eleventh century.

Artuqid rule eventually became divided among branches centered on **Mardin, Hisn Kayfa (Hasankeyf), and Harput**. These principalities occupied a strategically important region between the Byzantine Empire, the Crusader states, the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum, and the Muslim powers of Syria and Mesopotamia.

Najm al-Din Alpi belonged to the **Mardin branch** of the dynasty. During his reign, the Artuqids navigated a complex political environment in which military alliances, dynastic marriages, tribute arrangements, and commercial relationships were essential to maintaining regional autonomy.

The coin's most remarkable feature is its **appropriation of Byzantine Christian iconography**. The scene of an emperor receiving a crown from the Virgin Mary closely resembles imagery found on Byzantine coins and other imperial works of art. Such representations conveyed the idea that imperial authority was granted or sanctioned by divine power.

The appearance of this scene on an Islamic coin does not necessarily indicate Christian religious affiliation or devotion on the part of the issuing ruler. Rather, it illustrates the adaptability of established visual symbols within a region where Byzantine monetary traditions remained familiar and influential.

The two facing busts on the obverse similarly demonstrate the continued circulation of classical and Byzantine artistic conventions. The Artuqids were particularly notable for producing bronze coins with large, elaborate figural designs, including portraits, astrological imagery, animals, and scenes adapted from earlier civilizations.

This practice distinguished Artuqid coinage from many contemporary Islamic issues, which generally emphasized calligraphic inscriptions rather than human figures. The relatively large surfaces of Artuqid bronze dirhams allowed engravers to create complex compositions that combined political messages with striking visual imagery.

The **tamgha** on the obverse provides another layer of cultural significance. Such emblems originated in the traditions of Turkic and other Inner Asian peoples, where they could identify clans, lineages, or ruling houses. Its presence alongside Byzantine portraits and Arabic inscriptions reflects the Artuqids' position at the intersection of several cultural worlds.

Mardin itself was an important commercial and cultural center. Situated above the plains of northern Mesopotamia, the city controlled routes connecting the upper Tigris basin with the markets of Syria and Anatolia. Its population included Muslims and several Christian communities, whose artistic and architectural traditions contributed to the region's distinctive cultural character.

The Artuqids also became known for their contributions to architecture, engineering, and scholarship. During the following decades, the engineer **al-Jazari** worked at the Artuqid court in the region of Diyarbakir, producing his celebrated treatise on mechanical devices in 1206 CE. His work illustrates the technical and intellectual sophistication of the broader Artuqid cultural environment.

C023 is especially valuable within the **Medieval Anatolia** series because it demonstrates the extraordinary cultural diversity of the region during the twelfth century. In comparison with the Armenian Christian coinage of **C022**, this Artuqid dirham shows how neighboring Muslim rulers could adopt and reinterpret Christian imperial imagery while maintaining their own dynastic and religious identities.

The coin therefore offers a vivid example of **cultural exchange across religious and political boundaries**, illustrating how the visual language of money could transcend the identities of the rulers who issued it.
