This silver **grosso**, also known as a **matapan**, was issued by the **Republic of Venice** during the rule of **Doge Giovanni Soranzo (1312–1328 CE)**. The grosso was one of the most influential silver coins of medieval Europe, widely used in commercial transactions throughout the Mediterranean and beyond.

The coin was struck at the Venetian mint, known as the **Zecca**, in a city whose prosperity depended upon maritime trade, commercial finance, and an extensive network of overseas possessions and trading privileges.

The **obverse** depicts **Saint Mark**, the patron saint of Venice, presenting a ceremonial banner to the reigning doge. The inscription identifies Giovanni Soranzo and Saint Mark of Venice, while the letters **DVX**, meaning "duke" or "doge," appear vertically beside the banner.

The scene expresses a central principle of Venetian political ideology: the doge exercised authority as the elected leader of the republic, under the protection and symbolic sanction of Saint Mark. Unlike the hereditary monarchs of many contemporary European states, the doge was chosen through an elaborate electoral process and governed within a system of powerful councils and aristocratic institutions.

The **reverse** depicts **Christ Pantocrator**, or Christ the Almighty, seated upon a decorated throne. Christ raises his right hand in blessing and holds the Book of Gospels in his left. The abbreviated Greek letters **IC XC** identify Jesus Christ.

This religious imagery closely resembles the traditions of **Byzantine coinage**, reflecting the longstanding commercial and cultural connections between Venice and Constantinople.

The grosso typically weighed approximately **2.1–2.2 grams** and measured around **20–21 mm** in diameter, although the dimensions of this particular specimen have not yet been measured.

## Historical Significance

### The Rise of the Venetian Republic

The **Republic of Venice** emerged from communities established around the islands and lagoons of the northern Adriatic Sea during the early medieval period. Initially connected to the Byzantine Empire, Venice gradually developed its own political institutions and became an increasingly independent maritime power.

Its location at the northern end of the Adriatic provided access to trade routes connecting western Europe with the eastern Mediterranean. Venetian merchants transported timber, metals, textiles, spices, silk, and other commodities between European markets and the commercial centers of Byzantium and the Islamic world.

By the thirteenth century, Venice had developed into one of the wealthiest and most commercially influential cities in Europe.

The republic's political structure was unusual. Although the **doge** served as its ceremonial and executive head, his authority was constrained by the **Great Council**, the Senate, and other institutions dominated by the Venetian patriciate.

In **1297**, the process known as the *Serrata*, or closing of the Great Council, began consolidating political participation among established aristocratic families. By Giovanni Soranzo's reign, Venice was developing the increasingly oligarchic political system that would characterize much of its later history.

### Giovanni Soranzo and Fourteenth-Century Venice

**Giovanni Soranzo** was elected doge in **1312 CE**, succeeding Marino Zorzi. He governed during a period when Venice was consolidating its commercial position following political and military difficulties at the beginning of the fourteenth century.

The preceding reign of **Pietro Gradenigo** had been marked by conflict with the papacy over Venetian expansion into Ferrara. The resulting papal interdict disrupted the republic's political and commercial relationships.

Soranzo's reign was comparatively stable, allowing Venice to strengthen its economic institutions and maritime connections.

During this period, Venetian commerce extended across the Adriatic, Aegean, and Black Seas, while merchants maintained trading relationships with the Byzantine Empire, the Mamluk Sultanate, and other Mediterranean powers.

Venice also continued to develop its overseas territories and maritime infrastructure, reinforcing its position as a major intermediary between eastern and western markets.

Soranzo died in **1328 CE**, after approximately sixteen years as doge.

### The Venetian Grosso and Mediterranean Trade

The **Venetian grosso** was first introduced under **Doge Enrico Dandolo**, traditionally dated to approximately **1193–1205 CE**, as a relatively large and high-purity silver coin.

Its introduction responded to the growing demand for reliable silver currency suitable for expanding commercial transactions. Earlier medieval European silver pennies were often too small or too variable in value to serve conveniently in large-scale trade.

The grosso offered merchants a recognizable denomination with a comparatively stable silver content. Its consistent design and reputation contributed to its acceptance across the Mediterranean.

The coin became particularly important following the **Fourth Crusade of 1204 CE**, when Venetian forces participated in the capture of Constantinople and Venice acquired extensive commercial privileges and territorial possessions in the former Byzantine Empire.

Venetian influence subsequently extended across parts of the Aegean Sea, including Crete and important ports and islands along Mediterranean trade routes.

The grosso circulated within these commercial networks and inspired numerous imitations and related silver denominations in other medieval states.

The name **matapan** became associated with the Venetian grosso, although its precise etymology remains debated.

### Byzantine Influence on Venetian Coinage

The imagery of the Venetian grosso demonstrates the close relationship between Venetian and Byzantine artistic traditions.

The depiction of **Christ enthroned** follows established Byzantine conventions, particularly those found on imperial coins, mosaics, and religious icons.

Christ appears frontally, with a cruciform halo and a solemn expression, holding the Gospels and offering a gesture of blessing. This composition emphasizes his spiritual authority and the Christian character of the issuing state.

The obverse scene, showing Saint Mark presenting the banner to the doge, also draws upon Byzantine representations of rulers receiving authority from divine or saintly figures.

However, the Venetian adaptation expresses a distinct political message. Rather than depicting a hereditary emperor, the coin portrays the elected leader of a republic receiving the symbol of his office from the city's patron saint.

The design therefore combines **Byzantine religious symbolism with Venetian republican identity**.

### Saint Mark and Venetian Identity

**Saint Mark the Evangelist** occupied a central position in Venetian civic identity. According to Venetian tradition, his relics were brought from Alexandria to Venice in **828 CE**, where they became the focus of devotion at the Basilica of San Marco.

The saint subsequently became the republic's principal protector and political symbol.

The famous **winged Lion of Saint Mark**, often shown holding a Gospel book, appeared throughout Venetian art, architecture, flags, and later coinage.

On the grosso, Saint Mark appears in human form, personally presenting the banner of office to the doge. The image communicates the belief that the republic's political authority was exercised under the saint's protection.

This association helped reinforce Venice's distinctive identity as a Christian maritime republic whose legitimacy rested upon both religious tradition and civic institutions.

### A Coin of an International Trading Republic

C027 illustrates the growing importance of **commercially trusted silver currency** in medieval Europe.

Unlike coins whose significance was primarily regional, the Venetian grosso was designed for use within an extensive maritime trading network. Its consistent weight, recognizable imagery, and established reputation helped make it an important medium of exchange across political and cultural boundaries.

The coin also provides an interesting comparison with other pieces in the collection.

**C025**, the Byzantine Anonymous Class B follis, depicts Christ Pantocrator as the central symbol of imperial religious authority. The Venetian grosso adopts closely related imagery while expressing the political identity of an independent commercial republic.

**C026**, a Christian imitation of an Almohad square dirham, illustrates another form of cross-cultural monetary influence, in which Christian issuers reproduced the recognizable designs of Islamic currency.

Together, these coins demonstrate how **medieval monetary systems developed through trade, imitation, and cultural exchange**, often borrowing artistic conventions from neighboring civilizations.

C027 represents Venice at a time when its maritime commerce, political institutions, and increasingly international currency were establishing the foundations of one of Europe's most enduring and influential republics.
