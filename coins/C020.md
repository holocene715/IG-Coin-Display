This silver **dirham** was issued during the brief reign of **Ahmad Tekudar**, the third ruler of the Mongol **Ilkhanate**, which governed Persia and neighboring regions during the 13th and 14th centuries. Tekudar ruled from **1282 to 1284 CE**, succeeding his brother Abaqa Khan. The coin belongs to an early period of Ilkhanid coinage, when Mongolian political traditions and Islamic monetary conventions existed side by side.

The obverse features an **Old Uyghur inscription**, which was adopted by the Mongols during the early 13th century. The inscription identifies Ahmad's authority in relation to the Great Khan, reflecting the continuing importance of the wider Mongol imperial hierarchy. Beneath the main inscription, his name **Ahmad (احمد)** appears separately in Arabic, distinguishing this variety from other early Ilkhanid issues. The reverse bears the Islamic declaration of faith, or **shahada**, written in Arabic within a square border. Additional inscriptions surround the central design. The combination of Uyghur and Arabic writing on a single coin reflects the cultural and political complexity of the Ilkhanate, where a Mongol ruling dynasty governed a predominantly Muslim population with established traditions of Arabic-language administration and coinage.

The specimen is also notable for being **holed**, which may indicate that it was suspended, worn, or otherwise repurposed after its original use as currency.

## Historical Significance

The **Ilkhanate** was established in the mid-13th century following the Mongol conquests of western Asia. Its founder, **Hülegü Khan**, was a grandson of Genghis Khan and led Mongol campaigns into Persia, Mesopotamia, and surrounding territories. In **1258 CE**, his forces captured Baghdad and brought an end to the Abbasid caliphate's rule in the city. The Ilkhanate subsequently emerged as one of several major states formed from the fragmentation of the Mongol Empire.

Although the Mongols had conquered territories with long-established Islamic institutions, their early rulers maintained a variety of religious traditions, including Buddhism, forms of Christianity, and indigenous Mongolian beliefs. **Ahmad Tekudar**, a son of Hülegü, was particularly significant because he embraced Islam and adopted the Muslim name Ahmad. His accession in 1282 represented an early attempt to align the Ilkhanid monarchy more closely with the religious traditions of its Muslim subjects. Tekudar sought improved relations with the **Mamluk Sultanate of Egypt and Syria**, a major Muslim power that had repeatedly fought the Mongols. His religious and diplomatic policies, however, contributed to tensions within the Ilkhanid ruling elite. His nephew **Arghun** challenged his authority and ultimately overthrew him in **1284**, bringing his reign to an abrupt end.

The coin's inscriptions illustrate the **overlapping systems of political legitimacy** that characterized early Mongol rule. The Old Uyghur inscription connects Ahmad to the authority of the Great Khan and the broader Mongol imperial tradition. The Arabic inscription of his Muslim name and the Islamic profession of faith on the reverse communicate his authority within the religious and monetary conventions of the Islamic world.

C020 also provides a useful chronological comparison with **C017**, a Qarakhanid dirham from Kasan dated to approximately 1201–1202 CE. The two coins are separated by roughly eight decades, during which the Mongol conquests dramatically transformed the political geography of Central and western Asia. C017 belongs to the final decades of the pre-Mongol political order, while C020 represents the successor states that emerged from Mongol expansion.
