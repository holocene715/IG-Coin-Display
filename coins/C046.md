Struck at the Philadelphia Mint in 1817, this copper large cent represents the early coinage of the **United States of America** during the presidency of James Monroe. Its obverse depicts Liberty wearing a coronet, surrounded by thirteen stars representing the original states, while its reverse displays the denomination within a wreath. As one of the everyday coins of the young republic, the large cent reflects the development of a national monetary system during a period of territorial expansion, economic growth, and growing American political identity.

## Historical Background

Following the **American Revolution** and the ratification of the United States Constitution in 1788, the new federal government faced the challenge of establishing a reliable national currency. The **Coinage Act of 1792** created the United States Mint in Philadelphia and established a decimal monetary system based on the dollar, divided into one hundred cents. Before the development of a dependable domestic coin supply, Americans relied heavily on foreign coins, privately issued tokens, and barter for everyday transactions. The production of federal copper cents was an important step toward creating a unified monetary system and reinforcing the authority of the new government. Unlike the precious-metal coins used for larger transactions, copper cents were intended primarily for small purchases and ordinary commerce.

The **Coronet Head large cent** was introduced in 1816, replacing the earlier Classic Head design. Its obverse featured a left-facing representation of **Liberty**, wearing a coronet bearing her name, with thirteen stars surrounding the portrait. The design is generally attributed to *Robert Scot*, the chief engraver of the United States Mint, although the precise contributions of individual engravers to early American coin designs remain subjects of numismatic discussion. The reverse retained the established American motif of a wreath enclosing the denomination, surrounded by the name of the issuing nation. These classical and republican symbols deliberately avoided portraits of living rulers, distinguishing American coinage from the monarchical traditions common throughout Europe.

The year **1817** marked the beginning of the presidency of **James Monroe**, who took office in March following the administration of James Madison. The United States was emerging from the economic and political disruptions of the **War of 1812**, during which British military operations and wartime financial instability had challenged the young republic. The years following the war are often associated with the **Era of Good Feelings**, a period characterized by declining partisan competition at the national level and growing confidence in American independence. Nevertheless, the country faced substantial internal disagreements over banking, regional economic interests, slavery, and western expansion. The same year also saw the signing of the **Rush–Bagot Agreement**, which limited naval armaments on the Great Lakes and helped establish a more peaceful relationship between the United States and British North America.

The Philadelphia Mint struck approximately **3.95 million large cents dated 1817**, making the issue an important example of early American copper coinage. Two principal obverse varieties are recognized: the standard thirteen-star variety, represented by this specimen, and an unusual *fifteen-star variety* produced from a die with two additional stars. Although the thirteen-star design reflected the original states, the United States had grown to twenty states by the end of 1817, illustrating how the stars on early American coins could serve as historical symbols rather than a literal count of current states. Large cents were substantially larger and heavier than modern American pennies, with a standard weight of approximately 10.89 grams and a diameter of about 28 millimeters.

Throughout the early nineteenth century, copper large cents played an important role in everyday American commerce, circulating alongside silver and gold coins as well as foreign currency that remained common in the United States. Their production also reflected the gradual improvement of minting technology, die preparation, and federal monetary administration. The Coronet Head design continued through 1839, followed by the Braided Hair large cent, before the large copper cent was eventually replaced by the smaller Flying Eagle cent in 1857. This **1817 Coronet Head cent** represents an early stage in the development of American national coinage, connecting the ideals of the founding generation with the expanding economy and political institutions of the nineteenth-century republic.
