This small **billon jital** is attributed to **Mahipala**, a ruler or issuing authority associated with the medieval Rajput dynasties of northern India. Its two faces depict a recumbent bull and a mounted horseman, continuing one of the most recognizable coin designs of medieval South Asia.

The obverse shows a **zebu bull resting on the ground**, facing left, with a symbol above its rump. Around the animal is an inscription in **Nagari script**, conventionally read as *Śrī Mahipāla*, meaning "the illustrious Mahipala." The reverse features a highly stylized **horseman facing right**, holding a banner or standard. On many surviving examples, the horseman is reduced to an arrangement of angular shapes that can be difficult to interpret without comparison to better-preserved coins.

The coin was struck in **billon**, an alloy containing silver and a substantial proportion of base metal, usually copper. Its small size and relatively low intrinsic value are characteristic of the jital denomination, which circulated widely in parts of northern India and neighboring regions during the medieval period.

The attribution and date of this type remain uncertain. The Numista catalog assigns the Mahipala jital to approximately **1100–1200 CE**, while another specialist dealer places it around **1266–1300 CE**. These differing dates reflect the difficulties of identifying and dating some regional medieval Indian coinages, particularly when the surviving coins lack explicit dates or identifiable mint inscriptions.

The issuing mint is also unknown. Although the type is associated with **Rajputana**, the historical region corresponding broadly to parts of modern Rajasthan and neighboring areas, its precise place of production cannot be established from the available references. The name Mahipala appears in several Indian historical contexts, and the identification of the issuing authority should therefore be treated with caution.

## Historical Significance

The **bull-and-horseman coinage** of medieval northern India represents one of the region's most enduring monetary traditions. Its origins can be traced to the coinage of the **Hindu Shahi dynasties**, which ruled parts of present-day Afghanistan and northwestern India before the expansion of the Ghaznavids. Shahi silver and billon coins commonly depicted a recumbent bull on one side and a mounted horseman on the other, designs that became widely recognized across the region.

Following the decline of Shahi political power during the eleventh century, the bull-and-horseman design continued to be used by numerous successor authorities. These included regional Hindu rulers as well as Muslim dynasties that adapted existing monetary practices to facilitate commerce. Over time, the original designs became increasingly stylized, sometimes reaching a point where the animals and riders were represented by little more than conventional arrangements of lines and shapes.

C019 belongs to this broader **post-Shahi monetary tradition**. Its imagery demonstrates how a recognizable coin type could survive for centuries, even as political authority, religious affiliations, and regional boundaries changed. The continued use of the bull and horseman suggests that familiar designs played an important role in establishing the acceptability of coins within regional markets.

The **zebu bull** was a familiar and culturally important animal throughout much of the Indian subcontinent. Bulls were essential to agricultural production and also carried religious significance in Hindu traditions, particularly through their association with **Nandi**, the sacred bull of Shiva. Although the bull on these coins is sometimes interpreted in this religious context, the imagery also formed part of an established monetary convention and should not necessarily be understood as a direct statement of the issuing ruler's personal religious beliefs.

The mounted horseman represented another enduring element of South Asian royal and military imagery. Cavalry played a major role in warfare across northern India and Central Asia, and mounted figures appeared on coins issued by several different dynasties. Together, the bull and horseman became a visual pairing that connected successive generations of coinage to earlier regional traditions.

The **Nagari inscription** is particularly important for understanding this coin. The name *Mahipala*, preceded by the honorific *Śrī*, reflects the use of Sanskrit-derived royal language on medieval Indian currency. Unlike the Arabic inscriptions found on many contemporary Islamic coins, the Nagari lettering on C019 belongs to an indigenous South Asian writing tradition. Its inclusion in the collection illustrates the coexistence of distinct linguistic and monetary systems across medieval Eurasia.

During the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, northern India underwent major political transformations. Rajput dynasties governed numerous regional kingdoms, while the expansion of the **Ghurids** and later the **Delhi Sultanate** introduced new political structures and monetary practices. Despite these changes, local coin types often persisted, and the jital remained an important denomination in the monetary economy of northern India.

The name **jital** eventually became associated with a wide range of small silver, billon, and copper coins. These issues varied considerably in appearance and composition, reflecting the decentralized and changing monetary conditions of medieval South Asia. The Mahipala jital demonstrates how a relatively modest coin could carry forward an artistic and economic tradition with roots several centuries earlier.

Within this collection, C019 provides a striking contrast with **C018**, the Almohad square silver dirham of North Africa. Both coins belong broadly to the medieval period, but their designs represent very different approaches to expressing monetary authority. The Almohad dirham is dominated by Arabic religious inscriptions, while the Mahipala jital retains traditional animal and equestrian imagery alongside a short Nagari legend.

C019 also bridges the collection's earlier Central Asian coinage and the later Mongol issues. Its bull-and-horseman design reflects the long history of cultural and commercial interaction across the mountains and plains connecting Afghanistan, Central Asia, and northern India. As a result, the coin illustrates both the continuity of local South Asian traditions and the broader networks through which monetary ideas spread across medieval Eurasia.
