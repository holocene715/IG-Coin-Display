This small **billon jital** is attributed to **Mahipala**, a ruler or issuing authority associated with the medieval Rajput dynasties of northern India. Its two faces depict a **highly stylized** recumbent bull and a mounted horseman, continuing one of the most recognizable coin designs of medieval South Asia. This coin has been clipped, meaning portions of the edge of the coin's design are no longer present.

The obverse shows a **zebu bull resting on the ground**, facing left, with a symbol above its rump. Around the animal is an inscription in **Nagari script**, conventionally read as *Śrī Mahipāla*, meaning "the illustrious Mahipala." The reverse features a highly stylized **horseman facing right**, holding a banner or standard. On many surviving examples, the horseman is reduced to an arrangement of angular shapes that can be difficult to interpret without comparison to better-preserved coins. The issuing mint is also unknown but likely in the area of Delhi. 

## Historical Significance

The **bull-and-horseman coinage** of medieval northern India represents one of the region's most enduring monetary traditions. Its origins can be traced to the coinage of the **Hindu Shahi dynasties**, which ruled parts of present-day Afghanistan and northwestern India before the expansion of the Ghaznavids. Shahi silver and billon coins commonly depicted a recumbent bull on one side and a mounted horseman on the other, designs that became widely recognized across the region. Following the decline of Shahi political power during the 11th century, the bull-and-horseman design continued to be used by numerous successor authorities. These included regional Hindu rulers as well as Muslim dynasties that adapted existing monetary practices to facilitate commerce. Over time, the original designs *became increasingly stylized, sometimes reaching a point where the animals and riders were represented by little more than conventional arrangements of lines and shapes*.

The **zebu bull** was a familiar and culturally important animal throughout much of the Indian subcontinent. Bulls were essential to agricultural production and also carried religious significance in Hindu traditions, particularly through their association with **Nandi**, the sacred bull of Shiva. The mounted horseman represented South Asian royal and military imagery. Cavalry played a major role in warfare across northern India and Central Asia, and mounted figures appeared on coins issued by several different dynasties. 

The **Nagari inscription** is particularly important for understanding this coin. The name *Mahipala*, preceded by the honorific *Śrī*, reflects the use of Sanskrit-derived royal language on medieval Indian currency. Unlike the Arabic inscriptions found on many contemporary Islamic coins, the Nagari lettering on C019 belongs to an indigenous South Asian writing tradition. Its inclusion in the collection illustrates the coexistence of distinct linguistic and monetary systems across medieval Eurasia.

During the 12th and 13th centuries, northern India underwent major political transformations. Rajput dynasties governed numerous regional kingdoms, while the expansion of the **Ghurids** and later the **Delhi Sultanate** introduced new political structures and monetary practices. Despite these changes, local coin types often persisted, and the jital remained an important denomination in the monetary economy of northern India. The name **jital** eventually became associated with a wide range of small silver, billon, and copper coins. These issues varied considerably in appearance and composition, reflecting the decentralized and changing monetary conditions of medieval South Asia.
