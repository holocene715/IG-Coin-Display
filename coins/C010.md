This silver drachm was issued during the reign of **Bahram V**, one of the best-known rulers of the **Sasanian Empire**. Known as *Bahram Gur* in later Persian literature, he ruled from 420 to 438 CE and became the subject of an extensive literary tradition long after his death.

The obverse depicts Bahram in the highly distinctive style of Sasanian royal portraiture. His elaborate crown served as an important symbol of royal identity: Sasanian kings generally adopted individualized crown designs that allowed rulers to be distinguished visually on their coinage. Above the crown rises the large **korymbos**, a characteristic cloth-covered element of Sasanian royal dress. The surrounding inscription is written in **Middle Persian using the Pahlavi script**, marking a major linguistic departure from the Greek inscriptions seen on several earlier coins in this collection.

The reverse centers on a **Zoroastrian fire altar**, one of the defining images of Sasanian coinage. Two attendants stand on either side of the altar holding staffs, while a royal bust appears on the altar shaft. Fire held profound religious significance in Zoroastrian practice and became one of the most persistent symbols employed on Sasanian silver currency.

## Historical Significance

**Bahram V** ruled one of the major powers of Late Antiquity. By the fifth century CE, the Sasanian Empire controlled most of Iran and Mesopotamia and exercised influence across parts of Central Asia. It stood alongside the Roman and later Byzantine empires as one of the dominant states of western Eurasia. Bahram's reign included warfare with the Eastern Roman Empire as well as conflict along the empire's northeastern frontier, where Sasanian rulers faced increasingly powerful Central Asian groups.

Bahram V also became an unusually important figure in later **Persian cultural memory**. Medieval Persian literature transformed him into the legendary **Bahram Gur**, celebrated as a hunter, warrior, lover, and idealized king. Stories associated with him appear prominently in works including Ferdowsi's *Shahnameh* and Nizami's *Haft Paykar*. The historical ruler represented on this coin therefore became a literary figure whose reputation survived for centuries beyond the Sasanian Empire itself.

Within this collection, the coin marks an important stage in the transformation of the cultural world created after the conquests of Alexander the Great. Earlier Parthian, Indo-Parthian, Indo-Scythian, and Kushan rulers continued to employ **Greek language, Hellenistic royal titles, and Greek-derived imagery** long after Greek political control had disappeared. By the reign of Bahram V, those elements had largely vanished from official Sasanian coinage.

In their place was a distinctly **Iranian imperial visual language**. Middle Persian replaced Greek in the royal inscriptions, individualized Sasanian crowns replaced Hellenistic-style diademed portraits, and the Zoroastrian fire altar became the dominant reverse image. The coin therefore represents not simply the disappearance of Greek influence, but the emergence of a mature Sasanian political and religious identity that would itself exert enormous influence across the Near East and Central Asia.

The Sasanian monetary system was especially influential. Its broad, thin silver drachms circulated widely beyond the empire's borders, and their basic design was copied and adapted by neighboring peoples. After the Islamic conquest of Iran in the seventh century, early **Arab-Sasanian coinage** continued to reproduce Sasanian royal portraits and fire-altar designs before a distinctively Islamic coinage developed. In this sense, the Sasanian tradition represented by this drachm became another long-lived numismatic legacy in its own right.
