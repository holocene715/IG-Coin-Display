This silver **dirham** was issued during the reign of **Kaykhusraw II**, sultan of the **Seljuk Sultanate of Rûm**, which controlled much of Anatolia during the 12th and 13th centuries. Kaykhusraw ruled from **1237 to 1246 CE**, inheriting a powerful state that had reached its greatest territorial and economic strength under his father, **Kayqubad I**.

This specimen is attributed to the mint of **Konya**, the Seljuk capital in central Anatolia, and dates to approximately **1237–1243 CE**. Konya was one of the principal political, commercial, and cultural centers of the medieval Islamic world, situated along important routes connecting the Mediterranean coast, the Anatolian interior, and the cities of Persia and Central Asia.

The coin's most distinctive feature is the **Lion and Sun** motif on the obverse. A lion walks to the right beneath a radiant sun, which is sometimes represented with a human face. This striking composition combines animal and celestial imagery in a manner that distinguishes it from the predominantly calligraphic coinage of many contemporary Islamic states. The **reverse** contains Arabic inscriptions expressing the ruler's political and religious legitimacy, including reference to the Abbasid caliph. Although the coin was struck under an independent Seljuk ruler, references to the Abbasid caliphate reflected the continuing importance of the caliph as a source of religious authority within the Sunni Islamic world.


## Historical Significance

The **Seljuk Sultanate of Rûm** emerged following the expansion of the Seljuk Turks into Anatolia during the 11th century. Its name, *Rûm*, derives from the Arabic and Persian designation for the lands of the Romans, referring to territories formerly governed by the Eastern Roman, or Byzantine, Empire. Following the Byzantine defeat at the **Battle of Manzikert in 1071 CE**, Turkic migration and settlement transformed the political and cultural landscape of Anatolia. The Seljuks established a durable state that combined Turkic military traditions, Persian administrative practices, and Islamic religious institutions with the existing Byzantine and Armenian cultural heritage of the region.

By the early 13th century, the Sultanate of Rûm had become a major commercial and military power. Under **Kayqubad I (1220–1237 CE)**, the Seljuks expanded their influence, fortified cities, constructed caravanserais (like secure travel hotels/stables), and encouraged trade between the Mediterranean, Black Sea, and inland Asian markets. The capital at Konya developed into an important center of architecture, scholarship, and commerce.

**Kaykhusraw II** inherited this prosperous state in 1237 CE. His reign, however, was marked by increasing internal instability and the growing threat of Mongol expansion from the east. These pressures culminated in the **Battle of Köse Dağ in 1243 CE**, when Mongol forces decisively defeated the Seljuk army. The defeat transformed the political status of the Sultanate of Rûm. Although Seljuk rulers continued to govern Anatolia, they increasingly operated under Mongol overlordship, paying tribute and experiencing substantial interference in dynastic succession and state administration. Over the following decades, Seljuk authority weakened as regional Turkish principalities gained independence.

### The Lion and Sun Symbol

The Lion and Sun design is particularly significant because it illustrates the incorporation of **astrological and figural symbolism into Islamic coinage**.

The Lion and Sun subsequently became an enduring symbol in Persian and Iranian visual culture, appearing in different forms over many centuries and eventually becoming associated with the **Iranian monarchy**. The Seljuk coins of Kaykhusraw II represent an important early numismatic expression of this imagery, although they should not be understood as direct representations of its much later national significance.
