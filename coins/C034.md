## Historical Background

Frederick III (1609–1670) became King of Denmark and Norway in 1648, succeeding his father, **Christian IV**, one of Denmark's longest-reigning and most influential monarchs. Christian IV had overseen major architectural and commercial developments, including the expansion of Copenhagen and the founding of Christiania (modern Oslo), but his costly military campaigns against Sweden and involvement in the Thirty Years' War had weakened Denmark's finances and regional influence. Frederick inherited a kingdom burdened by debt and facing an increasingly powerful Swedish rival.

Frederick's accession coincided with the **Peace of Westphalia in 1648**, which brought an end to the Thirty Years' War and reshaped the political balance of Europe. Denmark–Norway remained an important Lutheran monarchy controlling strategic maritime passages between the North Sea and Baltic Sea, but Sweden had emerged as a leading northern European power. Frederick was elected king by the Danish nobility and initially required to accept significant restrictions on royal authority. The inscription **ELEC R** on this coin reflects his position as an elected monarch, a system that would undergo a dramatic transformation during his reign.

Frederick's greatest foreign-policy challenge was his rivalry with **Charles X Gustav of Sweden**, against whom Denmark declared war in 1657. The resulting conflict was disastrous for Denmark: Swedish forces crossed the frozen Danish straits and compelled Frederick to sign the **Treaty of Roskilde in 1658**, surrendering Scania, Halland, Blekinge, and other territories. When Sweden renewed its attack later that year, Frederick remained in Copenhagen during the Swedish siege, helping rally the city's defenders. Danish resistance, supported by Dutch naval intervention, prevented the Swedish conquest of the kingdom, and the subsequent Treaty of Copenhagen in 1660 restored some territories while confirming Sweden's possession of the southern Scandinavian provinces.

In the aftermath of these wars, Frederick achieved his most consequential domestic reform. Exploiting tensions between the nobility and other estates, he established **hereditary monarchy in 1660**, replacing the elective system under which he had received the crown. The transformation was formalized through the King's Law (*Konge loven*) of 1665, which established one of Europe's most extensive systems of royal absolutism. Frederick also strengthened the central administration and reduced the political independence of the traditional aristocracy, laying the foundations for the Danish absolute monarchy that endured until 1849.

This **1648 two-skilling coin**, cataloged as *Hede 122A*, was minted at the very beginning of Frederick III's reign, before the wars and constitutional changes that would define his legacy. Its crowned Danish arms and reference to an elected king represent a political order that would disappear within twelve years. The coin therefore captures a pivotal transition in Scandinavian history, as Denmark–Norway struggled to maintain its position in northern Europe while moving from aristocratic elective monarchy toward centralized hereditary rule.
