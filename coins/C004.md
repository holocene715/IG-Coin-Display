This small copper coin was struck at **Pergamum** (or Pergamon), capital of the Attalid Kingdom, during the reign of **Attalos II Philadelphos**. Pergamum was one of the most important political and cultural centers of the Hellenistic eastern Mediterranean and developed into a major center of Greek art, architecture, and learning under the Attalid dynasty.

The obverse depicts **Athena** wearing a helmet, while the reverse shows a **coiled serpent** with its head raised. The Greek inscription reads **ΦΙΛΕΤΑΙΡΟΥ**, “of Philetairos,” referring to **Philetairos**, founder of the Attalid dynasty. The continued use of his name by later Attalid rulers linked their coinage to the dynasty's founder and reinforced the legitimacy and continuity of Attalid rule.

## Historical Significance

**Attalos II Philadelphos** ruled Pergamon during the final decades of the independent Attalid kingdom. He had already played an important political and military role during the reign of his elder brother, Eumenes II, before becoming king in 159 BCE. The Attalids cultivated close relations with Rome while competing with neighboring Hellenistic states for influence in Asia Minor, making Pergamon an increasingly important regional power during the second century BCE.

The kingdom was also a major center of **Hellenistic culture and scholarship**. Pergamum's monumental architecture, sanctuary complexes, sculpture, and famous library made the city one of the leading cultural centers of the Greek-speaking world. Only a few decades after this coin was issued, the last Attalid king, **Attalos III**, bequeathed his kingdom to Rome in 133 BCE. Much of the territory subsequently became the Roman province of Asia, making the Attalid kingdom an important link between the Hellenistic political world and the expanding Roman presence in the eastern Mediterranean.
