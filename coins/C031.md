This **billon douzain**, valued at twelve deniers, is attributed to the papal coinage of **Clement VIII (1592–1605)**, issued in the *Comtat Venaissin* of southern France around **1593–1594 CE**. The coin belongs to a period when the papacy governed not only territories in central Italy but also the separate enclave of Avignon and its surrounding possessions in Provence. The type was issued during the administration of **Silvio Savelli**, who served as papal vice-legate from 1592 to 1594. Although Clement VIII ruled the Catholic Church from Rome, local papal officials administered Avignon and the Comtat Venaissin on his behalf.

The **douzain** was a denomination valued at **twelve deniers**, widely associated with French monetary systems of the late medieval and early modern periods. Its name derives from the French word *douze*, meaning twelve. Douzains were commonly produced in **billon**, an alloy containing a relatively small proportion of silver mixed with copper. This composition allowed monetary authorities to issue coins of modest value without requiring the substantial silver content of higher denominations.

The **obverse** originally displayed the papal coat of arms, surmounted by the tiara and crossed keys of Saint Peter. The surrounding inscription identified Clement VIII as Supreme Pontiff. The **reverse** featured a long cross with two roses and two lions arranged around it, accompanied by a Latin inscription identifying the papal vice-legate and the year of issue. The present specimen is **extremely worn**, with only the edge of the papal shield visible on the obverse and the central cross identifiable on the reverse. Its attribution to the 1593–1594 Savelli issue is therefore based on comparison with cataloged examples rather than surviving inscriptions or identifiable dates.


## Historical Significance

The city of **Avignon**, situated on the Rhône River in southern France, became one of the most important centers of papal government during the late Middle Ages. In **1309**, Pope Clement V established the papal court at Avignon, beginning a period during which successive popes governed the Catholic Church from the city rather than Rome. This era, known as the **Avignon Papacy**, lasted until **1377**, when Pope Gregory XI returned the papal court to Rome. Avignon remained under papal control long after the popes returned to Italy. The city had been purchased by Pope Clement VI in 1348, while the surrounding *Comtat Venaissin* had come under papal sovereignty in 1274. These territories formed a geographically separate possession of the Papal States, surrounded by lands associated with the Kingdom of France and the County of Provence.

**Clement VIII**, born Ippolito Aldobrandini in 1536, was elected pope in **1592** and reigned until his death in 1605. His pontificate occurred during the **Counter-Reformation**, the period of Catholic institutional reform and religious consolidation following the Protestant Reformation. The Council of Trent, which concluded in 1563, had established important reforms concerning Catholic doctrine, clerical education, ecclesiastical discipline, and religious practice. Clement VIII continued efforts to implement these reforms, strengthen papal administration, and reinforce Catholic institutions across Europe. He also played a significant diplomatic role in European affairs.

In **1595**, he granted absolution to **Henry IV of France**, who had converted from Protestantism to Catholicism in 1593. This reconciliation helped restore relations between the French monarchy and the papacy during the final stages of the French Wars of Religion. Clement's pontificate also saw the incorporation of the **Duchy of Ferrara** into the Papal States in 1598, strengthening papal territorial authority in northern Italy. This douzain was minted near the beginning of his reign, when religious conflict and political realignment continued to shape much of western Europe.

Papal rule over Avignon and the Comtat Venaissin continued well beyond the reign of Clement VIII. During the 17th and 18th centuries, the territory remained a distinctive papal enclave within the increasingly centralized French monarchy. The French crown periodically occupied Avignon during diplomatic disputes with the papacy, but the territory generally retained its separate political status. This arrangement came to an end during the **French Revolution**. In **1791**, Avignon and the Comtat Venaissin were annexed by revolutionary France, ending centuries of papal sovereignty in the region.

C031 has a direct thematic connection to **C028**, the mezzo giulio of **Pope Clement VII (1523–1534)**. Both coins were issued under the authority of the papacy during the 16th century, and both display papal heraldic imagery. However, they represent different aspects of papal government. C028 was issued at **Rome**, the principal seat of the papacy, and reflects the authority of a Medici pope during the turbulent political and religious developments of the early Reformation. C031 was issued in the **Comtat Venaissin**, a geographically separate papal possession administered through legates and vice-legates. The two coins thus demonstrate the papacy's dual identity as both the central institution of the Catholic Church and a territorial government with possessions extending beyond central Italy.
