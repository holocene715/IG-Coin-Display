This **silver two-kreuzer coin** was issued in **1574 CE** by the **Free Imperial City of Colmar**, a prosperous town in Alsace that belonged to the **Holy Roman Empire**. The coin was struck during the reign of **Maximilian II (1564–1576)**, Holy Roman Emperor and a member of the powerful Habsburg dynasty.

Although Colmar is located in modern-day France, during the sixteenth century it was a self-governing imperial city within the politically fragmented Holy Roman Empire. Its local government exercised considerable autonomy, including the right to issue coinage, while recognizing the overarching authority of the emperor.

The **obverse** displays Colmar's civic coat of arms, featuring a mace within a shield. The surrounding Latin inscription identifies the coin as money issued by the Imperial City of Colmar, while the date 1574 appears divided around the central design.

The **reverse** features the crowned, double-headed imperial eagle, a prominent symbol of the Holy Roman Empire. The numeral **2** appears on the eagle's breast, indicating the denomination of two kreuzer. The surrounding inscription identifies Emperor Maximilian II.

The combination of civic and imperial imagery reflects the distinctive political organization of the Holy Roman Empire, in which hundreds of cities, principalities, bishoprics, and other territories exercised varying degrees of independence under a shared imperial framework.

The present specimen weighs **1.90 g** and measures **23.8 mm** in diameter. Its surfaces show considerable circulation wear, and the irregular outline reflects its manufacture by hammering rather than modern mechanical striking. Both measurements are larger than those reported for the Numista reference example, making additional comparison with other specimens desirable.

## Historical Significance

### Colmar: A Free Imperial City

**Colmar**, situated in the fertile Upper Rhine Valley of Alsace, developed into an important commercial center during the Middle Ages.

Its location near the Rhine River placed it along major trade routes connecting the German-speaking territories of the Holy Roman Empire with Switzerland, Burgundy, and other parts of western Europe.

In **1226**, Colmar received the status of a **Free Imperial City**, placing it directly under the authority of the Holy Roman Emperor rather than a regional prince or feudal lord.

This status provided Colmar with significant privileges, including substantial control over its municipal government, commercial affairs, and local administration.

Imperial cities were not fully independent sovereign states in the modern sense. They remained members of the Holy Roman Empire and owed obligations to the emperor and imperial institutions. Nevertheless, their political autonomy distinguished them from towns governed by territorial princes.

By the sixteenth century, Colmar was one of several imperial cities in Alsace that maintained their own municipal institutions and commercial identities.

The city arms on the obverse of this coin express that civic autonomy. The coin was issued in the name of Colmar itself, rather than a local hereditary ruler.

### The Holy Roman Empire in the Sixteenth Century

The **Holy Roman Empire** was one of the most politically complex states in European history.

Rather than functioning as a centralized kingdom, it consisted of a vast collection of territories governed by emperors, princes, bishops, abbots, knights, and autonomous cities.

These territories possessed differing degrees of political independence and maintained their own laws, institutions, and traditions.

The emperor stood at the head of this system, but his authority depended heavily upon negotiation and cooperation with the empire's constituent states.

By the sixteenth century, the imperial title was closely associated with the **Habsburg dynasty**, whose extensive territories included lands in Central Europe and, through another branch of the family, Spain and its overseas possessions.

The imperial eagle on this coin represents Colmar's membership in this broader political order.

Its placement opposite the city's coat of arms illustrates the coexistence of two levels of authority: the municipal government that issued the coin and the emperor whose sovereignty it acknowledged.

### Maximilian II: Emperor During the Reformation

**Maximilian II** became Holy Roman Emperor in **1564**, succeeding his father, Ferdinand I.

His reign occurred during a period of profound religious division following the Protestant Reformation.

The spread of Lutheranism and other Protestant movements had challenged the religious unity of the empire, producing conflicts between Catholic and Protestant rulers and communities.

The **Peace of Augsburg in 1555** had established a framework for coexistence between Catholic and Lutheran imperial estates, but significant religious and political tensions remained unresolved.

Maximilian II was personally interested in religious reconciliation and generally pursued a comparatively conciliatory approach toward Protestantism, while remaining within the Catholic political establishment.

His efforts reflected the difficulties of governing an empire containing territories with different religious affiliations and considerable local autonomy.

The two-kreuzer coin was minted in **1574**, near the end of Maximilian's reign, only two years before his death in 1576.

Its imperial imagery therefore belongs to a period when the Habsburg emperors sought to preserve political cohesion across an increasingly religiously divided Central Europe.

### Alsace and the Protestant Reformation

The region of **Alsace** occupied an important position in the religious transformations of the sixteenth century.

Its towns were closely connected to the intellectual, commercial, and religious networks of the Upper Rhine, particularly the nearby cities of Strasbourg and Basel.

The Reformation spread through these networks during the early sixteenth century, influencing many Alsatian communities.

Colmar's own religious history was shaped by tensions between established Catholic institutions and growing Protestant influence.

In **1575**, only one year after this coin was struck, Colmar formally permitted Lutheran religious practice, marking an important development in the city's religious history.

The coin thus dates to a transitional moment immediately preceding the official establishment of Protestant worship in the city.

This local development reflected broader patterns throughout the Holy Roman Empire, where municipal governments frequently played important roles in determining the religious character of their communities.

### The Imperial Eagle and Civic Heraldry

The **double-headed eagle** was one of the most recognizable symbols of the Holy Roman Empire.

Its two heads had been used in imperial heraldry for centuries and came to represent the dignity and authority of the emperor.

By the sixteenth century, the crowned double-headed eagle appeared on a wide variety of imperial objects, including seals, coats of arms, buildings, and coins.

On C031, the eagle occupies the reverse, with the numeral **2** displayed prominently on its breast.

The obverse, by contrast, features the **coat of arms of Colmar**, traditionally depicting a mace.

This combination was characteristic of coinage issued by autonomous imperial cities. Local symbols identified the issuing community, while imperial symbols acknowledged the larger political structure to which it belonged.

Unlike coins bearing the portrait of a hereditary monarch, this issue emphasizes both the city's corporate identity and the authority of the empire.

The design provides a compact illustration of the decentralized political organization of sixteenth-century Central Europe.

### The Kreuzer and Regional Commerce

The **kreuzer** was a widely used monetary denomination across southern Germany, Austria, Switzerland, and neighboring regions of the Holy Roman Empire.

Its name derived from the German word *Kreuz*, meaning "cross," referring to the cross designs that appeared on earlier coins of this denomination.

By the sixteenth century, kreuzers formed an important component of the region's monetary system and were commonly used for relatively small commercial transactions.

The two-kreuzer denomination represented a modest value suitable for ordinary circulation.

The Holy Roman Empire's decentralized political structure resulted in a highly varied monetary landscape. Numerous cities, princes, and ecclesiastical territories issued their own coins, often according to regional standards and imperial monetary regulations.

Consequently, merchants and money changers regularly encountered coins bearing different local designs and issued by different authorities.

Colmar's position along important commercial routes made such regional monetary exchange especially relevant.

The production of a locally identified two-kreuzer coin reflects the city's participation in the interconnected economy of the Upper Rhine.

### Alsace Between the Empire and France

Although Colmar was an imperial city in 1574, its political affiliation changed significantly during the following century.

The **Thirty Years' War (1618–1648)** devastated much of Central Europe and fundamentally altered the political balance within the Holy Roman Empire.

The **Peace of Westphalia in 1648** expanded French rights and influence in Alsace, although the legal status of individual imperial cities remained complicated.

Colmar continued to retain elements of its traditional autonomy for several decades.

In **1673**, French forces occupied the city, and in **1679**, the Treaties of Nijmegen confirmed French sovereignty over Colmar.

The city's incorporation into France was part of a broader expansion of French power along the Rhine frontier under King Louis XIV.

This later history illustrates the changing political geography of Alsace, a region whose communities experienced successive periods of imperial, French, and German administration.

C031 was minted approximately a century before Colmar's incorporation into France, when the city still identified itself politically as an autonomous member of the Holy Roman Empire.

### A Coin Representing Imperial Decentralization

The 1574 two-kreuzer of Colmar represents a political system markedly different from the increasingly centralized territorial monarchies emerging elsewhere in Renaissance Europe.

While rulers such as **Cosimo I de' Medici** were consolidating hereditary control over expanding territorial states in Italy, cities such as Colmar continued to exercise substantial local autonomy within the Holy Roman Empire.

This makes C031 an interesting counterpart to **C029**, the 1546 crazia of Cosimo I.

Both coins feature heraldic imagery, but they express different political relationships.

The Florentine crazia prominently displays the arms of a ruling dynasty, emphasizing hereditary authority over the city.

The Colmar two-kreuzer displays municipal arms alongside the imperial eagle, emphasizing the city's corporate autonomy within a larger political framework.

Together, the coins illustrate the diversity of political institutions that existed in sixteenth-century Europe, even as broader trends toward territorial consolidation and centralized government were reshaping the continent.
