This **billon douzain**, valued at twelve deniers, is attributed to the papal coinage of **Clement VIII (1592–1605)**, issued in the **Comtat Venaissin** of southern France around **1593–1594 CE**. The coin belongs to a period when the papacy governed not only territories in central Italy but also the separate enclave of Avignon and its surrounding possessions in Provence.

The type was issued during the administration of **Silvio Savelli**, who served as papal vice-legate from 1592 to 1594. Although Clement VIII ruled the Catholic Church from Rome, local papal officials administered Avignon and the Comtat Venaissin on his behalf.

The **obverse** originally displayed the papal coat of arms, surmounted by the tiara and crossed keys of Saint Peter. The surrounding inscription identified Clement VIII as Supreme Pontiff.

The **reverse** featured a long cross with two roses and two lions arranged around it, accompanied by a Latin inscription identifying the papal vice-legate and the year of issue.

The present specimen is **extremely worn**, with only the edge of the papal shield visible on the obverse and the central cross identifiable on the reverse. Its attribution to the 1593–1594 Savelli issue is therefore based on comparison with cataloged examples rather than surviving inscriptions or identifiable dates.

The coin weighs **1.00 g** and measures **19.4 mm** in diameter. Both measurements are substantially smaller than those of the published reference example. Extensive circulation wear, clipping, or other alteration could contribute to these differences, although the precise explanation cannot be established from the surviving details.

## Historical Significance

### Avignon and the Papal Territories of Southern France

The city of **Avignon**, situated on the Rhône River in southern France, became one of the most important centers of papal government during the late Middle Ages.

In **1309**, Pope Clement V established the papal court at Avignon, beginning a period during which successive popes governed the Catholic Church from the city rather than Rome.

This era, known as the **Avignon Papacy**, lasted until **1377**, when Pope Gregory XI returned the papal court to Rome.

Avignon remained under papal control long after the popes returned to Italy. The city had been purchased by Pope Clement VI in 1348, while the surrounding **Comtat Venaissin** had come under papal sovereignty in 1274.

These territories formed a geographically separate possession of the Papal States, surrounded by lands associated with the Kingdom of France and the County of Provence.

Although the papacy's principal government operated from Rome, Avignon and the Comtat Venaissin continued to be administered as papal territories for centuries.

The douzain represented by C031 was issued more than two hundred years after the end of the Avignon Papacy, demonstrating the remarkable persistence of papal political authority in southern France.

### Clement VIII and the Counter-Reformation

**Clement VIII**, born Ippolito Aldobrandini in 1536, was elected pope in **1592** and reigned until his death in 1605.

His pontificate occurred during the **Counter-Reformation**, the period of Catholic institutional reform and religious consolidation following the Protestant Reformation.

The Council of Trent, which concluded in 1563, had established important reforms concerning Catholic doctrine, clerical education, ecclesiastical discipline, and religious practice.

Clement VIII continued efforts to implement these reforms, strengthen papal administration, and reinforce Catholic institutions across Europe.

He also played a significant diplomatic role in European affairs.

In **1595**, he granted absolution to **Henry IV of France**, who had converted from Protestantism to Catholicism in 1593. This reconciliation helped restore relations between the French monarchy and the papacy during the final stages of the French Wars of Religion.

Clement's pontificate also saw the incorporation of the **Duchy of Ferrara** into the Papal States in 1598, strengthening papal territorial authority in northern Italy.

The douzain was minted near the beginning of his reign, when religious conflict and political realignment continued to shape much of western Europe.

### Papal Government in Avignon

Because Avignon and the Comtat Venaissin were geographically separated from the main Papal States in Italy, their administration depended upon locally appointed papal officials.

The pope was represented by a **legate**, usually a senior ecclesiastical official who exercised political and administrative authority on behalf of the Holy See.

Much of the practical administration was conducted by a **vice-legate**, who oversaw governmental, judicial, and financial affairs.

The 1593–1594 douzain type is associated with **Silvio Savelli**, who served as vice-legate during the early years of Clement VIII's pontificate.

His name appears in the reverse inscription, linking the coin to the local administration responsible for its issue.

This arrangement illustrates how the papacy operated as both a religious institution and a territorial government.

The pope exercised spiritual authority over the Catholic Church, but in territories such as Avignon, papal officials also performed the functions of secular rulers, including taxation, judicial administration, and the issuance of currency.

### The Papal Coat of Arms

The obverse of the coin displays the heraldic symbols of the papacy.

The **crossed keys of Saint Peter** represent the authority traditionally granted to the apostle Peter and, by succession, to the popes.

In Christian symbolism, the keys are associated with the authority to bind and loose, as described in the Gospel of Matthew.

The **papal tiara**, historically a ceremonial crown worn by popes, symbolizes the dignity and authority of the papal office.

Together, the tiara and keys became among the most recognizable symbols of papal government and appeared frequently on coins, seals, monuments, and official documents.

The papal arms of Clement VIII were derived from the heraldry of the **Aldobrandini family**, a prominent Italian family whose members held important positions in ecclesiastical and political life.

Although only a small portion of the shield survives on the present specimen, its original appearance would have communicated the authority of Clement VIII over the territory in which the coin circulated.

### The Cross, Roses, and Lions

The reverse of the attributed type features a **long cross**, accompanied by two roses and two lions.

Crosses were among the most common religious symbols on medieval and early modern European coinage, particularly in territories governed by ecclesiastical authorities.

Their appearance on papal money reinforced the connection between the issuing government and the Catholic Church.

The roses and lions served as additional heraldic elements, distinguishing this issue from other denominations and types produced in Avignon and the Comtat Venaissin.

These symbols were not merely decorative. Heraldic devices allowed coin users to recognize issuing authorities and monetary types even when inscriptions were difficult to read.

On the present coin, the central cross remains the only clearly identifiable reverse feature.

The loss of the smaller heraldic elements and surrounding lettering illustrates the challenges of attributing heavily circulated small-denomination coins.

### The Douzain and Everyday Currency

The **douzain** was a denomination valued at **twelve deniers**, widely associated with French monetary systems of the late medieval and early modern periods.

Its name derives from the French word *douze*, meaning twelve.

Douzains were commonly produced in **billon**, an alloy containing a relatively small proportion of silver mixed with copper.

This composition allowed monetary authorities to issue coins of modest value without requiring the substantial silver content of higher denominations.

Small billon coins were important for ordinary transactions, including purchases in local markets and payments for everyday goods and services.

The use of a French monetary denomination in Avignon reflects the economic connections between the papal enclave and the surrounding regions of southern France.

Although Avignon was politically subject to the pope, its commerce was closely integrated with neighboring French communities.

The douzain therefore illustrates the distinction between political sovereignty and everyday economic geography.

### Avignon's Later Incorporation into France

Papal rule over Avignon and the Comtat Venaissin continued well beyond the reign of Clement VIII.

During the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the territory remained a distinctive papal enclave within the increasingly centralized French monarchy.

The French crown periodically occupied Avignon during diplomatic disputes with the papacy, but the territory generally retained its separate political status.

This arrangement came to an end during the **French Revolution**.

In **1791**, Avignon and the Comtat Venaissin were annexed by revolutionary France, ending centuries of papal sovereignty in the region.

The territory was subsequently incorporated into the administrative geography of modern France.

The coin represented by C031 therefore belongs to a long historical interval between the medieval Avignon Papacy and the revolutionary transformation of European political boundaries.

### A Connection to Earlier Papal Coinage

C031 has a direct thematic connection to **C028**, the mezzo giulio of **Pope Clement VII (1523–1534)**.

Both coins were issued under the authority of the papacy during the sixteenth century, and both display papal heraldic imagery.

However, they represent different aspects of papal government.

C028 was issued at **Rome**, the principal seat of the papacy, and reflects the authority of a Medici pope during the turbulent political and religious developments of the early Reformation.

C031 was issued in the **Comtat Venaissin**, a geographically separate papal possession administered through legates and vice-legates.

The two coins thus demonstrate the papacy's dual identity as both the central institution of the Catholic Church and a territorial government with possessions extending beyond central Italy.

They also illustrate the continued importance of papal coinage in an era when European political authority was becoming increasingly centralized, but regional jurisdictions and historical enclaves remained widespread.

The extreme wear of C031 provides a final reminder that these coins were not merely ceremonial expressions of religious authority. They were practical instruments of commerce, handled and exchanged in the ordinary economic life of papal Avignon and its surrounding territories.
