This *silver two-kreuzer coin* was issued in **1574 CE** by the **Free Imperial City of Colmar**, a prosperous town in Alsace that belonged to the **Holy Roman Empire**. The coin was struck during the reign of **Maximilian II (1564–1576)**, Holy Roman Emperor and a member of the powerful Habsburg dynasty. Although Colmar is located in modern-day France, during the 16th century it was a self-governing imperial city within the politically fragmented Holy Roman Empire. Its local government exercised considerable autonomy, including the right to issue coinage, while recognizing the overarching authority of the emperor.

The **obverse** displays Colmar's civic coat of arms, featuring a mace within a shield. The surrounding Latin inscription identifies the coin as money issued by the Imperial City of Colmar, while the date 1574 appears divided around the central design. The **reverse** features the crowned, double-headed imperial eagle, a prominent symbol of the Holy Roman Empire. The numeral **2** appears on the eagle's breast, indicating the denomination of two kreuzer. The surrounding inscription identifies Emperor Maximilian II.


## Historical Significance

**Colmar**, situated in the fertile region of Alsace, developed into an important commercial center during the Middle Ages. Its location near the Rhine River placed it along major trade routes connecting the German-speaking territories of the Holy Roman Empire with Switzerland, Burgundy, and other parts of western Europe. In **1226**, Colmar received the status of a **Free Imperial City**, placing it directly under the authority of the Holy Roman Emperor rather than a regional prince or feudal lord. This status provided Colmar with significant privileges, including substantial control over its municipal government, commercial affairs, and local administration. By the 16th century, Colmar was one of several imperial cities in Alsace that maintained their own municipal institutions and commercial identities.

The **Holy Roman Empire** was one of the most politically complex states in European history. It was neither Roman, a true empire...or holy. Rather than functioning as a centralized kingdom, it consisted of a vast collection of territories governed by emperors, princes, bishops, abbots, knights, and autonomous cities. These territories possessed differing degrees of political independence and maintained their own laws, institutions, and traditions. The emperor stood at the head of this system, but his authority depended heavily upon negotiation and cooperation with the empire's constituent states. By the 16th century, the imperial title was closely associated with the **Habsburg dynasty**, whose extensive territories included lands in Central Europe and, through another branch of the family, Spain and its overseas possessions.

**Maximilian II** became Holy Roman Emperor in **1564**, succeeding his father, Ferdinand I. His reign occurred during a period of profound religious division following the Protestant Reformation. The spread of Lutheranism and other Protestant movements had challenged the religious unity of the empire, producing conflicts between Catholic and Protestant rulers and communities. The **Peace of Augsburg in 1555** had established a framework for coexistence between Catholic and Lutheran imperial estates, but significant religious and political tensions remained unresolved. Maximilian II was personally interested in religious reconciliation and generally pursued a comparatively conciliatory approach toward Protestantism, while remaining within the Catholic political establishment. In **1575**, only one year after this coin was struck, Colmar formally permitted Lutheran religious practice, marking an important development in the city's religious history.

The **double-headed eagle** was one of the most recognizable symbols of the Holy Roman Empire. By the 16th century, the crowned double-headed eagle appeared on a wide variety of imperial objects, including seals, coats of arms, buildings, and coins. Unlike coins bearing the portrait of a hereditary monarch, this coin emphasizes both the city's corporate identity and the authority of the empire.
