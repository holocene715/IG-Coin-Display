Produced in Nuremberg in 1679, this brass accounting jeton commemorates the **Peace of Nijmegen**, a series of diplomatic agreements that ended the Franco-Dutch War and related European conflicts. Its obverse features an armored portrait of **Louis XIV of France**, while the reverse depicts marching cavalry beneath the inscription *PAX OMNIBVS*, meaning "Peace for All." Although manufactured in the Holy Roman Empire, the jeton celebrates a French monarch and an international diplomatic settlement, illustrating the widespread circulation of political imagery through Nuremberg's commercial token industry.

## Historical Background

**Louis XIV (1638–1715)** ruled France from 1643 until 1715, one of the longest reigns in European history. Known as the **Sun King**, he became the defining figure of French absolutism, consolidating royal authority, expanding the administrative power of the monarchy, and establishing the magnificent palace and court at Versailles. Under his rule, France emerged as Europe's most influential continental military power, supported by a large standing army, an increasingly centralized government, and ambitious territorial policies. Louis XIV's reign also witnessed important developments in French art, architecture, literature, and court culture, which became models for rulers throughout Europe.

The **Franco-Dutch War (1672–1678)** began when Louis XIV invaded the Dutch Republic, seeking to weaken a major commercial and political rival and expand French influence along the kingdom's northern frontier. The conflict soon grew into a broader European war as the Dutch secured support from Spain, the Holy Roman Emperor, and several German states. Despite early French successes, Dutch resistance and the formation of opposing alliances prevented Louis from achieving all his original objectives. The war was fought across the Low Countries, the Rhineland, and other parts of western Europe, causing substantial military and economic disruption.

The **Peace of Nijmegen**, negotiated through a series of treaties in 1678 and 1679, brought an end to the conflict between France and its principal opponents. France secured important territorial gains, including **Franche-Comté** from Spain and several fortified towns in the Spanish Netherlands. The agreements strengthened France's strategic position and reinforced Louis XIV's reputation as a powerful European ruler. However, the peace did not establish lasting stability. France's continued territorial ambitions and the concerns of neighboring states contributed to renewed tensions and further wars later in the century.

The reverse of this jeton depicts **military cavalry on the march**, an appropriate symbol for a peace settlement following years of warfare. Above the procession appears the Latin inscription *PAX OMNIBVS*, or "Peace for All," while the date **1679** appears below. The combination of military imagery and a proclamation of peace reflects the political messaging common in early modern commemorative objects: successful warfare and diplomatic settlement were presented as complementary expressions of royal power. The armored portrait of Louis XIV on the obverse reinforces the association between the French monarch, military achievement, and the restoration of peace.

Although the jeton commemorates a French diplomatic achievement, it was manufactured in **Nuremberg**, an Imperial Free City within the Holy Roman Empire. Nuremberg was internationally renowned for producing brass accounting tokens, or *Rechenpfennige*, which were used on counting boards and circulated widely through European commercial networks. Its craftsmen frequently reproduced portraits, heraldic symbols, and political events associated with foreign rulers, supplying a market that extended well beyond the empire's borders. Displayed alongside the **André Hac accounting jeton (C036)**, this 1679 issue demonstrates how German-made tokens could represent distinctly French subjects, from royal monetary administration to the military and diplomatic ambitions of Louis XIV.
